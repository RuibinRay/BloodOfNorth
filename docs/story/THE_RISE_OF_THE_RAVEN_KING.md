# Blood of the North: The Rise of the Raven King

Nov 12, 2026 · @Ray

## Characters

A farmer's son whose parents are murdered by a warlord grows up, gathers six companions and nine islands, kills the warlord's seven champions and two bodyguards, and becomes the Raven King of the North. Seven acts plus an epilogue; Eirik is 9 when it starts and 41 when it ends.

### Eirik's gifts from the lightning

On the spring night after his tenth birthday, lightning strikes the old birch while Eirik is climbing it to save his ravens. He lives. He wakes with four things he did not have before:

- The scar. A pale, forked line through his left eyebrow, across the eyelid, down to the cheekbone. The eye still sees, but it turns silver-grey in cold light.
- Raven's Voice. He can speak to his two ravens in thought. They answer in short words, smells and pictures, not sentences.
- Raven's Eye. He can close his eyes and see what the white raven Mjöll sees, as far as she can fly in a quarter of an hour. Rain, darkness and fear blur it.
- Stormguard. When death is a heartbeat away, the scar burns and time seems to slow. He sees the blow before it lands and blocks it. It only works when he is calm, it leaves him shaking and drained, and it cannot stop arrows from every side or save anyone but himself.

### The enemy

Jarl Håkon Ironwolf is the main villain: about 55 to 60, Ragnar's former shield-brother, ruler of Svartøy, the Black Isle. He truly believes the North needs one ruler held together by fear. He is a master of every weapon: sword and shield, spear, axe, bow, and his signature weapon Wolf's Judgment, a war hammer with an iron head ringed by spikes. His great weakness: he cannot understand loyalty that is given freely.

Jarl Sigurd Stonehand is the hidden mastermind: the rich leader of the Iron Circle, a secret league of jarls who paid for the raid that killed Eirik's parents. Not a fighter; he wins with silver, hostages and forged letters. Killed by Håkon in Act VI.

## Act I — The Burning Snow

Eirik's age: 9 to 10. Theme: everything can be taken in one night, and someone can still choose to give it back.

### Chapter 1 — Hrafnvik

Hrafnvik was six turf-roofed houses, a smoking-shed for fish and a stony field that grew more rocks than barley. Eirik was nine and believed it was the whole world.

His father Ragnar was a big, quiet man with a burn scar on his forearm and hands that could mend a net or a cartwheel without looking. He did not talk about where he had lived before Hrafnvik. When Eirik asked, Ragnar would say, "Somewhere with worse food," and Eirik's mother Ylva would laugh in a way that meant there was more to it.

Ylva knew every plant on the hillside. She could stop a nosebleed with yarrow and make a cough go away with a tea that tasted like a wet dog's ear. She also knew letters, real runes, and she taught them to Eirik in the ash of the hearth with a stick. No other farmer's wife in Hrafnvik could do that. Nobody asked why.

On the last warm day of autumn, Ragnar took Eirik to the woodpile and put the old axe in his hands for the first time. It was heavier than it looked, with a bearded iron head and a band of darker iron, a collar, wrapped around the top of the handle. Ravens were scratched into the steel.

"Her name is Raven-Feeder," Ragnar said.

"Why?"

"Because when I carried her, ravens never went hungry." He saw his son's face and softened. "That was a long time ago. Now she splits wood. That is a better job."

He taught Eirik to plant his feet, to let the axe fall instead of forcing it, to strike where the grain was weak. Then he said something Eirik would remember for twenty years.

"A blade may save your life, but wisdom decides when to draw it."

Eirik swung, missed the log and buried the axe in the chopping block. Ragnar laughed so hard he had to sit down.

"And another thing. I always strike from the left. Everyone who ever fought me knew it. Don't be like me. Change."

That evening a stranger came up the path: a grey-cloaked man with a war hammer slung across his back and a beard going white at the edges. Ragnar sent Eirik inside. Through a gap in the wall planks, Eirik heard pieces of an argument.

"...he knows you are on this coast, Ragnar. Go north. Tonight."

"With a woman, a boy, and winter one week away? You know what the mountains do in winter."

"I know what he does."

The stranger left before supper. Ylva barred the door, which she never did. Ragnar sat by the fire for a long time, turning the axe over in his hands and pressing his thumb against the iron collar, as if checking something hidden there was still safe.

Eirik fell asleep wondering who "he" was.

### Chapter 2 — Wolves in the Snow

The first snow came three nights later, soft and heavy. Eirik woke to the dogs barking, then to the dogs stopping.

Through the smoke hole he saw torches. Many torches. Men were coming over the ridge with wolf pelts on their shoulders and wolf heads painted on their shields.

Ragnar was already dressed. He did not look surprised. He took down a round shield Eirik had never seen him use and put Raven-Feeder in his belt. He knelt and held Eirik's face in both hands.

"Listen. Your mother will take you out the back. You go up to the old birch on the ridge, then over, then down to the river. You do not stop. You do not come back. Not for me, not for her, not for anything."

"Father—"

"Say it."

"I do not come back."

Ragnar kissed his forehead and went out the front door to meet thirty men alone.

Eirik saw some of it from the back field. His father fought like nothing he had ever imagined: the shield rising, the axe flashing from the left, always from the left, two raiders falling in the snow, then a third. The raiders gave way and a man stepped through them, tall and broad, a grey wolf skin over his mail and a helmet with iron eye-guards.

Ragnar lowered his axe a little. "Håkon," he said. "Brother."

"You should have gone north," said the wolf-man.

They fought. It did not last long. Ragnar was a farmer now, and Håkon had never stopped being a warrior. When Ragnar fell, the axe flew out of his hand and landed in the snow near the woodpile.

Ylva did not run with Eirik. She ran back. She pulled Raven-Feeder out of the snow and shoved it into Eirik's arms. "Your father's. Don't let them have it. Go!"

A raider came at them, a young, thin-faced man with a hand axe. Ylva had her herb knife. She slashed and opened his right hand from knuckles to wrist, and his blood sprayed bright red on the snow. He screamed, swapped hands and struck her down.

That raider was Kettil. From that night his men called him Red-Hand, and he told everyone it was because of how many he had killed.

Eirik ran. He ran with the axe dragging in the snow behind him, up the hill, past the old birch, over the ridge. Kettil chased him, cursing and bleeding. At the river the ice was thin and black. Eirik crossed it on his belly the way his father had taught him. Kettil stepped on it standing up, went through to his waist, and had to crawl back out half frozen.

When Kettil reached the camp, shaking and blue, Håkon asked him one question.

"The boy?"

Kettil had lost a child and nearly lost his own life to a farmer's wife with a cooking knife. He was not going to admit any more failure tonight.

"Through the ice, my lord. He is under the river."

Håkon nodded and turned away. Behind him, a quiet, well-dressed man in a fur cap counted silver into a raider's palm. His name was Sigurd Stonehand, and he had paid for this night. He looked at the burning farm the way a merchant looks at a sale that has closed.

### Chapter 3 — The Winter Alone

Eirik lived through that winter, though there were many days he did not expect to.

He found a hollow under a fallen spruce on the far side of the ridge and lined it with branches. He tried to make fire with a flint from his mother's pouch and failed for two days. On the third day he managed it, under the old birch where the wind could not reach, and he sat over the tiny flame and cried for the first time since the raid. He cried until he was sick. Then he fed the fire.

He ate whatever he could find. Frozen lingonberries, bitter and wrinkled. Tree bark boiled soft. A dead hare he found half eaten by foxes, already crawling with white grubs that had survived inside it. He picked out what he could, cooked the rest until it was black, and ate it. He threw up twice. The third time it stayed down.

He chewed his leather belt when there was nothing else. He learned that birch bark burns even when wet, that snow melted in the mouth steals warmth from the body, that you must never sleep with wet feet.

The she-wolf found him in midwinter.

She was thin and grey and limping. For three nights she circled his camp just outside the firelight, her eyes catching the flames. On the fourth night she came close enough that he could smell her. Eirik stood up with Raven-Feeder in both hands, too heavy for him to swing properly, and a burning branch in the other.

They stared at each other. Then he saw why she limped. Her back leg was caught in a trapper's wire snare, and the wire had cut into the bone. She was starving for the same reason he was: she could not hunt.

Eirik could have killed her. He had the axe. She was weak. Her meat would feed him for a week.

He thought of the wolf-men on the ridge, of the wolf painted on their shields, and of how much he wanted to kill something.

Then he thought of his mother running back for the axe.

He threw the last of his hare bones to the far side of the fire. While the wolf ate, he crept around behind her and cut the wire with the axe. She snapped at him, missed, and limped off into the dark.

She did not come back. But for the rest of that winter, no other wolf came near his hollow either.

### Chapter 4 — The Bear's House

Bjorn found him at the first thaw.

Bjorn was hunting elk on the ridge with two dogs and an enormous spear. He was the largest man Eirik had ever seen, red-bearded and broad as a door. The dogs found the boy first: a filthy, bone-thin creature curled under a spruce, hugging an axe nearly as long as he was.

Bjorn crouched down. "Are you a troll?"

Eirik said nothing.

"Trolls are usually bigger," said Bjorn. "And they smell better. You smell like a dead fox wrapped in another dead fox."

Eirik still said nothing. Bjorn took off his own cloak, wrapped the boy in it, axe and all, picked him up with one arm and carried him home.

Home was Bjornstead, half a day's walk south along the coast: twenty houses, a longhouse with carved dragon heads on the gables, a little harbor, a forge and fields that ran down to the sea. Bjorn's wife Signe took one look at Eirik and started heating water. She scrubbed him in a tub three times. The water was black after the first scrub, brown after the second, and only grey after the third. She burned his clothes. She combed lice out of his hair with a bone comb and cracked them between her thumbnails, one by one, while humming.

Their daughter Freydis, who was eleven, watched from the bench.

"Is he staying?"

"He's staying," said Signe.

"Does he talk?"

"Not yet."

"Good," said Freydis. "Then I'll talk for both of us."

Eirik did not speak for forty days. When he finally did, it was to Freydis, who had just told him for the hundredth time that he looked like a drowned owl.

"You look like a goat," he said.

Freydis was so delighted she ran to tell her mother. "He talks! He insulted me!"

That night, when the house was asleep, Eirik pried up a flat stone beside the hearth, dug a hollow, wrapped Raven-Feeder in an oiled sheepskin and buried it. He laid the stone back down. He did not want to see it. He did not want to give it away. He wanted it there, under the fire, where it would stay warm.

Bjorn heard every bit of it from his bed and said nothing, then or ever.

### Chapter 5 — Two Ravens

That spring a storm knocked a raven's nest out of the cliff pines above the harbor. Freydis and Eirik found it in the wet grass: one chick dead, two alive. One was black. The other was white as frost, with pale grey eyes.

"A white raven," breathed Freydis. "We have to show Old Hrefna."

Old Hrefna was Bjornstead's herb-woman and, some said, a seeress. She lived in a hut that smelled of goat, smoke and something dead in a pot. She looked at the white chick for a long time.

"Odin had two ravens," she said. "Thought and Memory. They flew over the world every day and told him all they saw." She looked at Eirik. "This one will see a great deal. Feed her well."

Feeding baby ravens turned out to be disgusting. They screamed every hour, day and night. They ate worms, beetles, fish guts and minced liver, and they would only eat it if you pushed it down their open throats with a finger. Freydis refused after the first day. Eirik did it for two months. He named the white one Mjöll, fresh snow, and the black one Sót, soot.

Sót bit him every single day. Mjöll sat on his shoulder and pulled gently at his hair.

By late summer they could fly. Everyone expected them to leave. They did not. They roosted in the old birch on the hill behind Bjornstead, which was almost the twin of the old birch above Hrafnvik, and came down when Eirik whistled.

That autumn Old Hrefna cast her rune-bones on a calfskin for the children, as a game. She was quiet for a long time, then said strange things, half to herself: "A yew that sings in a storm. A sword that sings over graves. A winter tooth. Two brothers. A star that falls and is beaten into a hammer." She swept up the bones and would not say what it meant. Freydis said she was making it up. Eirik was not so sure.

### Chapter 6 — The Lightning

The spring after his tenth birthday, a storm came off the sea so fast that the fishing boats barely made it home.

Eirik was in the high field with the sheep when the sky went green-black. The birch on the hill was thrashing. In its crown, Mjöll and Sót were clinging to a branch that was cracking at the trunk.

He should have run home. Instead he ran up the hill and started climbing.

The bark was slick with rain. The wind tore at him. He reached the branch, grabbed it, and called to the ravens, and the white one hopped toward his hand.

The world turned white.

There was no sound at first. Then a sound bigger than the sea. Then nothing.

Bjorn found him at the foot of the split, smoking tree. The birch had been cut down the middle like a log under an axe. Eirik lay in the wet grass with both ravens standing on his chest, screaming at anyone who came near. His shirt was burned. On his back and arm was a pattern of red lines like the branches of a tree. Across his left eye, from brow to cheekbone, ran a deep, forked burn.

He was in fever for three days. Signe and Old Hrefna took turns at his bed. Hrefna packed the eye with honey and moss. Signe said prayers to Thor, then to Frigg, then to every god she could name, then shouted at all of them.

On the third night Eirik heard a voice.

It was not a voice exactly. It was a feeling of feathers and wind, and a single thought that was not his.

Warm. Boy. Wake.

He opened his good eye. Mjöll was perched on the bedpost, looking at him.

Wake, she said again, without opening her beak.

He closed his eyes, and suddenly he was looking down at the top of his own head from the bedpost: a pale, thin boy with a bandaged face, and Signe asleep on a stool beside him. He gasped. The picture vanished.

Over the next weeks he learned what the lightning had done. He could hear his ravens and speak back to them in his mind. Mjöll's words were simple: fish, cold, man, danger, home. Sót's were mostly rude. When Eirik closed his eyes and reached for Mjöll, he could see what she saw, fields and roofs and the harbor from high above, for as long as he could hold the thread. Holding it too long gave him a headache like an axe in the skull.

His left eye healed. It still saw, but in cold light the iris looked silver, and the scar never faded.

The last gift showed itself in early summer. Bjorn was teaching him to fight with a stick and a little shield, as every boy in Bjornstead was taught. Bjorn feinted high, then swung low and hard from behind, a trick that had dropped grown men at the harvest games.

Eirik's scar burned like a coal. Everything slowed. He saw the stick coming the way you see a leaf falling. He turned, raised the little shield, and the blow cracked against it.

Then his knees gave out and he sat down in the grass, gasping and white.

Bjorn stared at him for a long time. Then he went and fetched Old Hrefna.

She listened, nodded and named it. "Stormguard. The sky put a little of itself in you. It will guard you when death comes close, and only then, and only if you keep your head. It will cost you every time." She leaned close. "And it will not guard anyone else. Remember that. Gifts from the sky draw eyes from the earth. Tell no one."

They told no one. In Bjornstead, people said the Bjornsson boy had been touched by Thor and had a funny eye, and that his pet ravens were a bit odd, and left it at that.

### Chapter 7 — The Wolf's Hall

Far out across the water, on the Black Isle of Svartøy, Håkon Ironwolf sat in his hall and listened to the reports of a dozen raids. His fleet grew every year. Island kings paid him tribute so he would leave them alone. Sigurd Stonehand's silver bought him ships and the loyalty of men who loved silver more than anything.

Håkon gathered seven champions around him: the cruelest, strongest and most skilled warriors in the North. He gave each one a seat at his table and a wolf brooch of black iron. He chose two more to stand behind his chair: Vigdis, the fastest blade anyone had ever seen, and Gorm, a man so large and so hard to kill that he had once walked home from a battle with three arrows in his back. People called them Håkon's left hand and right hand.

Kettil Red-Hand, his right hand ruined and his left hand now his fighting hand, got the lowest seat at the champions' table. He never forgot it.

And on the mainland, ten winters passed.

Eirik grew tall and strong splitting wood, hauling nets and lifting Bjornstead's stones. He learned to read the sea and the sky. He learned to fight with spear and shield from Bjorn, and to win arguments from Freydis, mostly by losing them. He learned that a whole village can become your family if you show up when the roof needs fixing.

He never dug up the axe.

End of Act I.

## Act II — The Farmer's Axe

Eirik's age: 19. Theme: a peaceful life is worth defending.

### Chapter 1 — Ten Winters

The act opens on a summer morning so beautiful it almost hurts. Sheep graze by the stream. Smoke rises straight up from twenty roofs. Gulls argue over the fishing boats.

Eirik, nineteen now and taller than most men in Bjornstead, is splitting firewood. Bjorn, greyer and heavier, leans on the fence and watches.

"You swing that axe as though the tree insulted your mother."

"Maybe it did."

Bjorn takes the axe from him, sets a log and splits it with a lazy flick. "Strength is useful. Knowing where to put it is better."

Behind them, the village goat, Old Skeggi, is eating Bjorn's left boot, which Bjorn had taken off to shake out a stone. By the time anyone notices, only the laces are left. Bjorn chases the goat around the yard with one bare foot while the whole village laughs. Skeggi escapes onto the longhouse roof and finishes the laces there.

Freydis, twenty-one and running half of Bjornstead's trade, walks past with a basket of dyed wool and a sly look.

"You have frightened every wolf in the forest, but you still cannot speak to a girl at the harvest feast."

"I speak to you."

"That explains your problem."

### Chapter 2 — A Farmer's Life

Life in Bjornstead is hard work and small joys. Eirik hunts, fishes, cuts turf, herds sheep up to the summer pastures and rides Bjorn's stocky grey horse along the coast road to trade with neighboring farms.

On an elk hunt in the birch forest, he sends Mjöll up through the trees and closes his eyes. Her thoughts come in pieces.

Water. Blood. Broken branch.

He follows the pictures to a stream where a wounded elk has fallen, shot by some other hunter who never found it. It feeds the village for a week.

There is a dispute at the local Thing, the open-air assembly where free men settle arguments. Two neighbors, Hauk and Grjotgard, both claim a red cow. Hauk says it was born in his barn; Grjotgard says it wandered onto his land and ate his barley, so now it is his by right of damages. The lawspeaker recites old laws for an hour. In the end Bjorn rules that Grjotgard gets the cow's next calf as payment for the barley and Hauk keeps the cow. Both men leave unhappy, which Bjorn says means it was a fair judgment.

The harvest feast is the best night of the year. There is roast mutton, barley bread, smoked salmon, ale and mead. There is also kaestur, a shark that was buried in gravel for three months until it rotted enough to be safe to eat. It smells like a latrine pit that has died. Grown warriors pretend to love it. Freydis dares Eirik to eat a piece. He does, keeps a perfectly calm face, walks behind the longhouse and throws up into the pig trough. The pigs eat that too.

Bjorn challenges Eirik to a wrestling match, as he does every year. This year Eirik could win. Everyone can see it. At the last moment he lets Bjorn throw him.

Bjorn helps him up and mutters, "Next time you show mercy to an old man, make it less obvious."

The villagers roar.

That night Eirik looks up at the old birch behind the village. His scar begins to ache. A storm is coming. He does not know yet that it is not a storm of weather.

### Chapter 3 — Smoke on the Ridge

At dawn Mjöll comes back from the river road with one word.

Wolves.

Eirik closes his eyes and sees through her: seven armed men coming along the road, wolf heads painted on their shields. The man in front rides a black horse. He holds the reins in his left hand. His right hand is a twisted, scarred claw.

They ride into Bjornstead and the leader announces himself: Kettil Red-Hand, champion of Jarl Håkon Ironwolf. He says Bjornstead has not paid its tribute: a share of its harvest, silver and livestock that Håkon now claims from every village on this coast.

Bjorn says Bjornstead has never paid Håkon anything because it has never belonged to Håkon. He offers a gift of dried fish anyway, to keep the peace.

Kettil laughs. He demands twenty sheep, half the grain store and two young villagers to come with him as bonded thralls until the debt is paid. He points at two girls by the well.

Bjorn refuses. Kettil hits him across the face with the back of his ruined hand.

Eirik steps between them before he has decided to.

Kettil looks at him, bored, and then his eyes stop on the scar over Eirik's left eye. Something moves in his face.

"You look like a boy I once saw running through the snow."

Eirik goes cold to his fingertips.

Kettil smiles with half his teeth. "That boy was supposed to die."

They ride away. Everybody in Bjornstead knows they will be back.

### Chapter 4 — The Farmer's Axe

They come back at sunset with thirty men.

Fire arrows hit the thatch. The barley field goes up in a sheet of flame. Villagers run toward the longhouse. Raiders kick in doors and drag out anything valuable.

Eirik runs home. He kneels by the hearth, puts his fingers under the flat stone he has not touched in ten years and lifts it.

The sheepskin has rotted, but the oil kept the axe clean. Raven-Feeder lies in the hollow, the ravens on its blade dark with age.

For a moment everything goes quiet. He is nine years old on a woodpile and his father is laughing. A blade may save your life, but wisdom decides when to draw it.

He takes the axe and Bjorn's spare shield and walks outside.

Kettil is in the yard on his black horse. He sees the axe and his face changes. "Who are you supposed to be?"

"The boy who survived."

The fight for Bjornstead goes in waves. Bjorn fights at Eirik's side with his big bearded axe, bellowing like his namesake. Freydis and Signe pull the old and young into the longhouse and bar the doors. Freydis knocks a raider off the longhouse steps with a well bucket.

Eirik uses everything he knows. He slams a barn door into three men at once. He drags a raider into the pigsty mud, where the man slips and cannot get up. He fights in the narrow lane between the smithy and the smokehouse, where only one enemy can reach him at a time. Sót dives screaming at a raider's face and steals the man's helmet strap so the helmet falls over his eyes.

Then Kettil comes at him.

Kettil is fast and nasty. He fights with a hand axe in his left hand and a small iron buckler strapped to his ruined right forearm, hooking and smashing. He knocks Raven-Feeder out of Eirik's hands with a twist of the buckler. He kicks Eirik down into the mud and lifts his axe.

The scar burns. The world slows to a crawl.

Stormguard.

Eirik sees the axe coming down as clearly as a falling leaf. He rolls, brings the shield up, catches the blow and rams the shield rim into Kettil's knee. Kettil goes down. Eirik snatches Raven-Feeder from the mud and stands over him. Kettil is on his back, blood in his mouth, hands up.

"Wait. Wait! I can tell you who—"

Eirik raises the axe.

Behind him, the barn roof cracks. Children are screaming inside. Six of them had hidden in the hayloft, and the fire has reached it.

Eirik looks down at the man who killed his mother. Then he turns his back on him and runs into the burning barn.

He gets all six out. His hair is singed and his hands are blistered. When he comes back into the yard, Kettil is gone. He has left a trail of blood to the river, and in the mud where he lay is the black iron wolf brooch of Håkon's champions.

The raiders are fleeing. Bjornstead has lost four people, a third of its harvest and two houses. It has kept its children.

Freydis finds Eirik sitting on the well with the brooch in his hand.

"You had him," she says.

"I had him."

"Why didn't you—"

"There were children in the barn."

She sits down beside him and puts her head on his shoulder. Neither of them says anything else.

### Chapter 5 — The Wolf Remembers

On Svartøy, Kettil limps into Håkon's hall. Håkon is eating with his seven champions. Behind his high seat stand Vigdis Quickblood, thin and still as a blade, and Gorm Stone-Lung, who blocks out the light from the doorway.

Kettil kneels. "My lord. The farming village was stronger than expected."

Håkon keeps eating.

"There was a boy. A man now. A scar over his left eye. He carries Ragnar's axe."

Håkon stops chewing. The hall goes silent.

"Ragnar had no living son."

"I thought the same, my lord."

"Ten years ago you told me the boy was under the river." Håkon stands and walks slowly toward him. "Did you see him die?"

"No, my lord."

"Then why are you still speaking?"

Håkon takes the eating knife from his belt and cuts the wolf emblem from Kettil's cloak. "You will earn this back."

At the end of the table, Ulf Two-Blades snorts into his ale. Arne Longshot does not look up from fletching an arrow. Bersi Black-Anvil, huge and bald, keeps tearing at a lamb leg. Rurik Shieldsplitter is the only one who looks troubled.

Sigurd Stonehand, who happens to be visiting with a chest of silver, speaks softly. "Ragnar's son would be Halfdan's grandson. That is a problem for all of us."

"I know whose grandson he would be," says Håkon.

That night Håkon gives his orders. A bounty: the boy's weight in hacksilver for his head. Spies to every market on the coast. He does not send his army. Not yet.

"I want to know what he is," Håkon tells Vigdis. "A lucky farmer with a sharp axe, or something worse."

"And if he is something worse?" Vigdis asks.

"Then we cut the roots before the tree grows."

### Chapter 6 — The Peddlers

Two weeks later, two brothers arrive in Bjornstead with a cart of combs, needles, glass beads and cheap knives. Their names are Grim and Grimolf. They are friendly, they give the children honey cakes and they ask a lot of questions about the young man with the scar.

Mjöll does not like them. Smell wrong. Iron under cloth.

That night Eirik sends her to watch their camp. Through her eyes he sees them sharpening long knives and passing a skin of wine, laughing about how much silver a head weighs. Under their tarpaulin is a coil of rope and a sack big enough for a man.

They come for him at midnight. Eirik is waiting, but so is Freydis, who refused to stay in bed. The first brother creeps into the house and Freydis hits him in the face with Signe's iron frying pan. It makes a sound like a hammer on an anvil. The second brother runs, gets tangled in a fishing net that Eirik strung across the path, and is sat on by Bjorn until he confesses everything.

The brothers are tried at the Thing the next morning. They are declared outlaws, which in the North means anyone may kill them and no one may help them. Bjorn puts them on a leaky boat with two oars and a loaf of bread and tells them to row.

The village laughs about the frying pan for years. Freydis keeps it on a peg by the door and calls it Skull-Ringer.

But that night Bjorn sits up with Eirik.

"There's a price on you."

"I know."

"And you know why?"

Eirik shakes his head. "I've never known why. My father was a farmer."

Bjorn looks at the fire for a long time. "A farmer who fought like a king's guard. Who owned an axe like that." He sighs. "I don't know either, son. But I know Håkon doesn't spend silver on farmers."

### Chapter 7 — Walls Before Winter

Eirik calls the whole village to the Thing stones. It is the first time he has ever spoken in front of everyone, and his voice shakes.

Some people want to leave Bjornstead and scatter to relatives inland. Some want to send a gift of silver to Håkon and beg for peace. A few, quietly, think it would be easiest to hand Eirik over.

Eirik listens to every one of them. Then he says:

"I won't ask anyone to fight for me. If you want me to leave, I'll leave tonight. But if I go, Håkon's men will still come. He wants tribute from every village on this coast. We cannot promise that no enemy will ever come. But we can promise they will not find us sleeping."

Bjorn backs him. "And we'll need food as much as walls. A village that starves behind a wall is just a grave with a fence."

The vote is close. Bjornstead votes to stay and build.

What follows is the hardest summer anyone can remember. They cut timber in the hills and drag it down with horses and oxen. They dig a ditch around the whole village and sharpen stakes for its bottom. They raise a palisade twice a man's height with a fighting walk inside. They build a watchtower on the hill by the old birch, where Mjöll and Sót roost.

The work is not glorious. Eirik spends three days digging a new latrine trench because the old one is inside the planned wall line, and moving the old one involves shoveling ten years of village waste into barrows. Hauk the cow-owner faints from the smell. Freydis organizes the work rotas, the food and the tool repairs, and keeps a tally of who works and who hides. Signe dries and salts fish until her hands crack. Bjorn trains every man and woman who can hold a spear, making them form a shield wall in the pasture until they can do it in their sleep.

By the first frost, Bjornstead has walls.

### Chapter 8 — The Captain Without a Ship

They need iron, salt and grain for winter, and the nearest great market is Kaupang, four days' sail down the coast. Eirik goes on a fisherman's boat with Freydis's list and a pouch of silver.

Kaupang is the biggest place he has ever seen: hundreds of houses, ships from Denmark and Frisia and the far east, merchants selling glass, wine, swords and people. In a pen by the harbor, thralls with iron collars wait to be sold. Eirik has heard of slavery all his life. Seeing it is different. A girl his sister's age looks at him through the fence and does not look away.

On the quay, a man is shouting at the harbor reeve.

He is about Eirik's age, lean, with a short gold beard, a gold earring and two blades on his belt: a long seax on one hip and a short sword on the other. He is pointing at a beautiful sleek longship tied to the pier.

"That ship is mine! I built half of her myself!"

The reeve, a fat man named Orm with rings on every finger, is unmoved. "That ship belongs to the harbor until you pay your debts, Leif. Mooring fees. Gambling debts. The silver you borrowed from Thorkel's widow—"

"Borrowed is a strong word."

The man sees Eirik watching and instantly brightens. "You! You look like a man of taste. Would you like to buy a ship?"

Eirik looks at the ship. "Does it float?"

"Of course."

"Does it sail?"

"Usually."

"Does it belong to you?"

Leif hesitates. "That depends on how strictly you define belonging."

Eirik walks away. He does not expect to see Leif again.

That evening he finds out the truth from a drunk sailor in a tavern. Leif lost the crew's wages gambling and borrowed to cover it. But Orm the reeve rigged the dice games. He wants the ship for himself, and he plans to sell Leif's three youngest sailors, the ones who cannot pay their share, at the thrall market in four days.

Eirik goes back to the quay. Leif is sitting on a barrel drinking alone.

"Your crew is going to be sold," Eirik says.

Leif's face falls. "I know."

"Then why are you drinking?"

"Because I have no idea what else to do."

They make a plan. A merchant knarr sank last spring on the rocks off the Skerries with a cargo of Frankish silver. Nobody dares dive it, because the wreck lies in a sea cave the tide floods twice a day. Leif knows the waters. Eirik has a raven who can see the tide line from above.

They borrow a small boat. Mjöll tells Eirik when the water is turning. They dive the wreck in the freezing green dark, hauling up silver in a basket. Leif is a wonderful diver and a terrible thief: he keeps stuffing coins into his own shirt instead of the basket.

On the last dive the tide turns early. Gisli, Leif's youngest sailor, sixteen and the strongest swimmer, is caught by the rushing water and pinned under a beam inside the cave. Eirik sees him go under. The basket of silver is on a ledge beside him, the richest haul of the day.

Eirik lets go of the basket and swims for Gisli.

He gets the beam off and drags him up into the air. Gisli lives, coughing up seawater and seaweed. The basket of silver is gone forever into the cave.

Leif stares at him, dripping. "You left the silver."

"The man was drowning."

"Yes, but there was a great deal of silver."

Eirik just looks at him.

Leif opens his mouth, closes it, and then starts laughing. "I suppose this is why people keep following you."

What they did recover is enough. Eirik goes to the Kaupang Thing with Leif, the ship's builder's marks and the drunk sailor as a witness. The lawspeaker rules that Orm's dice were weighted. Orm must cancel the gambling debts, pay a fine and free the sailors. Leif pays his real debts with the wreck silver, including every coin he took from Thorkel's widow, though it makes him visibly ill.

The ship is his again. But Leif has no money for a crew, no cargo and no home port.

"Come to Bjornstead," Eirik says. "We have a harbor. We need a ship. You need a place nobody is trying to sell you."

Leif considers. "I'll stay until my debt to you is paid. Then we'll see."

"You don't owe me anything."

"That," says Leif, "is exactly why I'm worried."

He names the ship Sea-Raven, after Mjöll. On the voyage home Mjöll lands on his shoulder and pulls his earring. Leif swears she is trying to steal it. Sót steals it.

### Chapter 9 — The First Oath

Bjornstead celebrates the Sea-Raven's arrival with a feast. Leif tells the story of the sea cave in a way that makes him the hero, the silver three times bigger and the cave full of sea serpents. Gisli corrects him at every sentence. The whole village loves him by the end of the night.

Old Skeggi the goat eats part of the Sea-Raven's spare sail. Leif tries to have the goat put on trial at the Thing for theft. Bjorn rules that the goat is not a free man and therefore cannot be sued.

Late in the evening, Bjorn stands up and calls Eirik forward. He slides a plain silver arm-ring onto Eirik's arm.

"Not because you are my son. You already are. Because today, people chose to follow you."

The villagers cheer. Leif cheers loudest, then quietly checks how much the ring weighs.

That night Mjöll comes back from a long flight over the sea. Through her eyes Eirik sees it: far to the west, on an island he has only heard of, smoke is rising.

End of Act II.

## Act III — The Wolves of Iron

Eirik's age: 20 to 21. Theme: courage means protecting people who cannot repay you.

### Chapter 1 — The Island Prince

The smoke Mjöll saw was a burned fishing hamlet on Jarnøy, the Iron Isle, hit by Håkon's tribute collectors. In spring, the Sea-Raven sails there to trade Bjornstead's wool and salted fish for iron.

Jarnøy is a hard, beautiful place: black cliffs, red streams stained by iron ore, smelting furnaces smoking day and night on the hillsides. Its people are tough and proud. Its iron is the best in the North; a Jarnøy blade bends before it breaks.

Its ruler, King Hroald Ironvein, is fifty, grey and tired. He has kept Jarnøy safe for twenty years by paying Håkon a little every year so that Håkon does not take everything. His son, Prince Arvid, is seventeen and hates every coin of it.

On their second day in the harbor town, Eirik and Leif watch it happen. Four of Håkon's collectors are taking the season's tribute. One of them grabs a girl named Hallgerd, a fisherman's daughter, and says her father's debt will be paid by her service on Svartøy. Everyone knows what that means. Her father begs. The collector laughs and pulls her toward the boat.

Arvid steps out of the crowd with a sword. The collector draws his axe. It is over in three strokes. The collector is dead on the stones, and the wolf seal of Håkon Ironwolf hangs from his belt.

The other three collectors run for their boat. Eirik could stop them. He does not; killing them would only make things worse. They row away fast.

King Hroald is white with fury. "You have given a wolf an excuse to enter our house."

Arvid, still shaking, shouts back. "Should I have watched him take her?"

Hroald has no answer.

Nine days later a messenger arrives from Svartøy with Håkon's demand. Jarnøy will hand over Prince Arvid for judgment, and a hundred bars of iron as blood-price. If it refuses, Jarnøy will burn.

Hroald will not give up his son. But he has only a hundred trained fighters, and his scouts say Håkon is gathering two hundred.

Eirik has seen how Håkon's men treat villages. He makes a promise he is not sure he can keep. "I'll go home and ask my people for help."

### Chapter 2 — Fifty from Ravenhold

By now, sailors along the coast have started calling Bjornstead something else. Two ravens circle above its walls every day, and a white one is something nobody forgets. They call it the ravens' hold: Ravenhold. Bjorn complains about this for a month, then gives up and carves a raven on the gate.

He calls the people to the Thing stones. Eirik tells them what he saw on Jarnøy.

The argument is fierce. They survived Kettil's raid only a year ago. Their walls are new. Their harvest is thin. Hauk shouts that his son is not going to die for some island king who never helped anyone on the mainland.

Then Bjorn stands up, and when Bjorn stands up, people go quiet.

"If we wait until every island is burning, there will be no one left to help us when our own fires begin. Håkon eats one village at a time. He's counting on each of us thinking we're not next."

He proposes sending fifty of their best. Volunteers only. Eirik will lead them.

Hauk's son Ari, fourteen and mad with excitement, volunteers first. Bjorn tells him he is too young and sends him home to his mother. Ari sulks for a week.

The village throws itself into preparation. There are not enough weapons for fifty. There is not enough of anything.

That is when three strangers arrive in Ravenhold, one after another, in the same month.

The first is Brokk. He comes up the coast road on a mule with a cart of tools, an anvil chained to it and a crushed left hand that he can barely close. He is old, bald on top and bearded to his belt. He heard there was a village building walls and needing iron work, and he wants paying.

Within a day he has taken over Ravenhold's forge and insulted the village smith so badly the man goes fishing instead. Within a week he has made forty spearheads and repaired thirty shields. Every night at the fire he explains how he lost the use of his hand. On Monday a troll bit it. On Tuesday he caught a falling anvil to save a princess. On Wednesday a jealous husband slammed a door on it. Nobody ever learns the truth.

When he sees Raven-Feeder he goes completely silent and turns it over in his good hand for a long time.

"Whoever made this axe knew his iron."

"My father."

Brokk taps the dark iron collar at the top of the handle and frowns at it. "Then your father knew more than farming."

The second stranger is Astrid. Eirik finds her rather than the other way round. Mjöll keeps showing him a woman in the hills, alone, following the trail of a band of raiders who have been snatching people from outlying farms. When Eirik catches up with her, she has an arrow on the string pointed at his throat before he hears her move.

She is about his age, lean, with a braid of dark hair and a face that does not smile easily. Håkon's men burned her family's farm three years ago. Her parents died. Her little brother, Kári, was taken. She has been hunting Håkon's raiders ever since, one at a time.

Eirik asks her to join the fifty. She laughs at him.

"I have seen men promise justice before. Usually they mean killing the people they dislike."

He does not argue. Instead he helps her. Together they track the raiders to a camp in a ravine, where nine captured farmers are tied up to be sold. Mjöll shows them where the guards are. Astrid shoots two before they can shout. Eirik takes the rest. They free the farmers. Astrid looks for her brother among them. He is not there. Then she waits for Eirik to ask for something in return: silver, thanks, her bow.

He just helps the farmers home.

The next morning Astrid walks into Ravenhold with her bow on her back. "I know Jarnøy's forests," she says. "You'll need a guide."

The third stranger is Torvald. He arrives on a big brown horse with a war hammer slung across his back and a grey cloak, his beard going white at the edges. He asks for Eirik by name.

"I fought beside your father when we were young," he says. "I heard Ragnar's son was alive and fighting Håkon. I'd like to help."

Eirik stares at him. Something about the hammer and the cloak tugs at an old memory, a voice through wall planks, but it slips away before he can catch it.

Torvald is everything a young commander needs. He knows formations, supply, how to keep fifty frightened farmers in a shield wall. He trains them every morning until they can lock shields in the dark. He tells Eirik stories about Ragnar as a young warrior, and Eirik drinks them in like water.

Only once does something seem strange. Torvald asks, casually, whether Eirik's mother left him anything. A ring, a brooch, a letter? Eirik says no, only the axe. Torvald nods and changes the subject. He never looks at Raven-Feeder for long.

### Chapter 3 — The Healer's Hands

The fifty sail to Jarnøy in the Sea-Raven and two fishing boats. Leif discovers that half of Ravenhold's warriors get seasick. He spends the whole voyage stepping around them and complaining about the smell.

On Jarnøy they meet King Hroald's daughter, Princess Ingrid.

She is nothing like her brother. Where Arvid is fire, she is cold water: calm, quick and impossible to rattle. She has learned medicine from the island's healers and from Frankish and Irish travelers, and she is turning her father's feasting hall into a hospital for the coming battle. Straw is laid out for beds. Linen is being boiled. Honey, moss, yarrow and wine are lined up for wounds.

Leif comes in carrying a sailor who has cut his foot on a rock. He sees Ingrid and stands up a little straighter.

"I have survived seventeen sea battles."

Ingrid examines the sailor's foot without looking at Leif. "And how many of those were caused by you?"

Leif pauses. "That is an unfair question."

"Fetch clean water. The well, not the harbor. If you bring me harbor water I will pour it on you."

Leif fetches the water. He brings it from the well. Then he hangs around the hospital for the rest of the day, getting in the way, until she gives him a job rolling bandages just to keep his hands busy.

Astrid watches all this from the doorway with the closest thing to a smile anyone has seen on her face.

### Chapter 4 — The Battle of Iron Shore

The defenders number one hundred and fifty: fifty from Ravenhold and a hundred from Jarnøy. Håkon's force, Astrid's scouts report, is about two hundred, and it is led by two champions.

Arne Longshot, the archer, a lean, patient man who has killed more people from a hundred paces than most warriors have killed at arm's length.

Ulf Two-Blades, who fights with a sword in each hand and has never been beaten in a duel.

Eirik and Torvald spend six days preparing. They dig a trench across the beach and line it with sharpened stakes. Brokk and the Jarnøy smiths work day and night at the furnaces, making spearheads and arrowheads. Astrid places archers in the forest where she knows the attackers must pass.

Leif has the most disgusting idea of the campaign. Jarnøy's harbor town has a huge midden pit full of fish guts, rotten kelp and the town's latrine waste. Leif suggests they dig a second, shallow trench at the far end of the beach, fill it with the midden's contents and cover it with a thin layer of sand and seaweed.

"Why?" asks Ingrid, horrified.

"Because when two hundred angry men charge across a beach, the ones in front should get a surprise."

Torvald thinks about it and laughs. They call it the Stinking Ditch.

The enemy fleet appears at dawn: eleven longships out of the mist.

First Arne's archers land on the wooded headland and start shooting down into the town from the ridge. Arrows fall like rain. Astrid shouts for shields up. The defenders crouch under their shields, arrows thudding into the linden wood. Eirik closes his eyes and sends Mjöll over the ridge. Through her eyes he sees every archer behind every tree, and he calls their positions to Astrid. Her archers start shooting back with deadly accuracy.

Then Ulf's warriors charge across the beach.

The front rank hits the Stinking Ditch. The sand gives way and a dozen men go in up to their waists. The smell rolls across the battlefield like a wall. Men retch. Some stop fighting entirely to be sick. One warrior climbs out, sees what he is covered in, and simply runs back to his ship. The defenders cheer. For about a minute, it is the funniest thing anyone has ever seen.

Then the rest come over the stakes and the real fighting begins.

It is chaos and noise. The shield walls crash together. Torvald holds the center with his hammer, steady as a rock. Arvid fights like a madman to prove his father wrong. A wooden bridge over the town stream collapses under the weight of struggling men. A granary catches fire and the smoke covers everything.

Then a Jarnøy man, a fisherman whose wife and children live outside the wall, hears screams from the outer houses, panics and opens the side gate to go to them. Enemy warriors pour through.

Eirik has to choose: hold the shield wall at the beach, or go back and plug the gate and get the families out of the outer houses.

He leaves Torvald in command of the wall and takes ten men to the gate. They hold it, but it costs them. Grjotgard, the Ravenhold farmer who once argued over a red cow, takes a spear through the side covering the families' escape. He dies in the street with Hauk holding him.

"The calf," Grjotgard whispers. "You keep the calf."

"I'll give it to your wife," Hauk says. "You stubborn fool. I'll give her the cow too."

### Chapter 5 — The Longshot

Arne Longshot has climbed into the town's stone watchtower. From there he can see and shoot anywhere in the battle. Every few breaths, another defender falls with one of his black-feathered arrows in him. Astrid tries to shoot him, but the tower's narrow window protects him; she hits the stone a hand's width from his face, twice.

Eirik sends Mjöll circling the tower. She shows him a way up: through the burning granary, up a collapsed roof beam, across to a ledge on the tower's back wall where there is no window.

He goes through the fire with his cloak over his head. He climbs the beam with the heat blistering his hands. He reaches the ledge and comes in through the top hatch.

Arne is waiting for him. He knew. He has strung tripwires across the floor and hung a net of fishhooks in the doorway. He shoots at Eirik at point-blank range. Stormguard flares and Eirik catches the arrow on his shield; the point punches through the wood a finger's length from his eye. Arne drops the bow and pulls a seax and throwing knives. He fights like a cornered cat, slashing and darting away, trying to get back to his bow.

Arne glances up at the white raven circling outside the window. "The wolf said you could see what birds see."

"Then he should have told you I could see you."

Eirik corners him by the window. Arne goes for his last throwing knife. Eirik is faster. Raven-Feeder takes Arne Longshot in the chest, and the archer falls back through the window into the burning granary.

The first of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

Below, the attackers see Arne fall. Without his arrows, their attack loses its shape. The defenders begin pushing them back toward the beach.

### Chapter 6 — The Enemy Who Escaped

Eirik finds Ulf Two-Blades near the harbor, cutting his way toward the iron stores.

Ulf is a different kind of fighter from anyone Eirik has faced. He is fast, terrifyingly fast, attacking with both swords at once, changing direction mid-swing, laughing the whole time. Eirik is pushed back, cut on the arm, then the thigh. Stormguard saves him twice, and each time it leaves him weaker.

Ulf can tell. "Getting tired, farmer?"

Then Ulf does something cold. He shouts an order, and his men throw torches into three fishing boats tied at the pier. The boats are full of women, children and old people who were hiding there to escape by sea.

The boats catch fire. People scream.

Ulf smiles at Eirik and backs away toward his longship. "Choose."

Eirik chooses the boats. He and Leif cut the mooring lines and pull people out of the burning hulls and into the water. They get everyone out. When Eirik looks up, Ulf is standing in the stern of his longship as it rows out of the harbor, both swords raised in salute.

"Next time," Ulf shouts across the water, "I will not run."

The battle is won. Håkon's survivors escape in five of their eleven ships. But the cost is terrible. Thirty-one of Jarnøy's fighters are dead and nine of Ravenhold's. The town is half burned.

That night, the dead are laid on pyres on the beach with their weapons and a little food for the road to the next world. The fires burn until morning.

In front of everyone, Arvid kneels before his father and apologizes.

Hroald pulls him up. "You were right to protect the girl. But being right does not make the dead return."

Eirik hears it and never forgets it: even the right decision can have a price, and someone else often pays it.

### Chapter 7 — The Gift of Iron

King Hroald holds a feast for the defenders. He offers Eirik a chest of gold.

Eirik takes a little, enough to pay the families of the nine Ravenhold dead their full weregild, the blood-price owed for a life. Then he asks for something more useful: iron. Tools. And a few of Jarnøy's best smiths to come to Ravenhold for a year and teach.

Hroald stares at him. Then he laughs, for the first time in weeks. "You turn down gold and ask for work. Your father raised you strangely."

Twelve carts of Jarnøy's finest iron bars and ore go aboard the ships to Ravenhold.

Back home, Brokk is like a child at midwinter. He and the Jarnøy smiths build a second forge and work through the autumn. Every Ravenhold warrior gets a proper helmet, a better shield and a spear with a Jarnøy head. The best fighters get mail shirts, thousands of tiny iron rings riveted together, each shirt a month's work.

Brokk reforges Raven-Feeder for the first time, folding Jarnøy iron into its edge. He does not touch the dark iron collar at the top of the handle. "Some things you leave alone until they're ready to talk," he says.

And he makes something for Astrid. On the hill by the old birch, the tree the lightning split, a young yew had grown up through the scorched stump. The storm had cracked it too. Brokk and a Jarnøy bowyer cut a stave from it and make a bow that hums when the string is drawn, like wind in rigging. Astrid fires one arrow from it, clean through a fence post at a hundred paces, and does not speak for a long moment.

She names it Storm-Yew. Old Hrefna, when she hears, just nods. A yew that sings in a storm.

Ingrid asks her father for permission to go with Eirik's people. Ravenhold has no proper healer except Old Hrefna, and Hrefna's cures mostly involve goat urine. Hroald does not want to let her go. But he has seen her work through the night after the battle, and he understands that she can save more lives out there than in his hall.

She joins Eirik's companions.

Leif is delighted.

Astrid tells him, "She likes you about as much as a fisherman likes rotten bait."

Leif answers, "Then I have somewhere to begin."

### Chapter 8 — The Price of Victory

Ulf Two-Blades returns to Svartøy with five ships out of eleven.

Håkon listens to his report without moving. Arne Longshot is dead. Jarnøy is not punished. And Ragnar's son led the defense.

Worse, much worse: people are talking. In Solvik's markets, on Kinn's fishing boats, at Gulen's law-rock, men are saying that a farmer from the mainland beat two of Håkon's champions with fifty villagers. That Håkon can bleed.

Sigurd Stonehand advises sending the whole fleet to burn Ravenhold to the ground.

Håkon shakes his head. "Kill the boy and another hero will grow from his grave. They'll make a saga of him." He looks at the map of the islands carved into his table. "Destroy the roots, and the tree falls."

"The roots?" Sigurd asks.

"Every king who might help him. Every island that might feed him. When he has no friends left, he'll come to me himself."

Ulf asks to be sent back after Eirik. Håkon refuses. "You ran."

"I chose to retreat."

"Then choose to wait."

Ulf goes to the harbor and practices with his swords until dark, cutting a straw man to pieces again and again.

End of Act III.

## Act IV — Blood Across the Sea

Eirik's age: 22 to 23. Theme: trust is a leader's greatest strength, and his greatest weakness.

### Chapter 1 — The Golden Voyage

Two quiet years pass. Ravenhold grows. The Jarnøy smiths teach a dozen apprentices. The Sea-Raven carries trade up and down the coast. Freydis builds a proper market by the harbor and charges every merchant a small fee, which Leif calls robbery and Freydis calls taxes. Ingrid sets up a healing house and fights a long, bitter war with Old Hrefna over the use of goat urine. Torvald drills the village warriors every morning, and Eirik learns hammer-work, formations and battlefield command from him. He comes to love the old man like an uncle.

Then a ship arrives from Solvik, the Golden Quay, the richest of the islands.

King Eystein Goldhand is sending a great cargo ship to the court of King Alfred of Wessex in England: silver, worked Jarnøy iron, amber from the eastern sea, walrus ivory, furs, fine cloth and a gift of gold arm-rings for the English king. It is the most valuable cargo Solvik has ever sent. The ship is a huge, fat-bellied knarr called Fafnir's Bed, named after the dragon who slept on gold.

Eystein is afraid Håkon will take it. He offers Ravenhold a fortune to help guard it.

Eirik agrees. Ten of Ravenhold's best go with him, along with forty of Solvik's own guards, fifty armed escorts in all. Leif commands the Sea-Raven as the escort ship. Astrid, Ingrid and Torvald come along. Brokk stays home because, he says, "the sea is for fish and fools."

Ari Hauksson, sixteen now and taller than his father, begs to come as a ship's boy. Eirik says yes. Hauk is not happy. Ari is so excited he cannot sleep for two nights.

The first days at sea are wonderful. Sailors sing rowing songs. Merchants argue about prices. Leif teaches Ari to read the swell and to use a sunstone, a clear crystal that shows where the sun is through cloud.

The days are also disgusting. There are no private places on a ship. Everyone relieves themselves over the side while a friend holds their belt. The bilge stinks of old fish, vomit and wet wool. By the fourth day everyone has lice. The salted herring is green at the edges. Ingrid makes everyone wash their hands in seawater before eating, and the sailors think she has gone mad.

Leif plays dice every evening and wins every game. Ingrid watches him for three nights, then picks up his dice and drops them in a cup of water. They both roll the same way, every time, because they are weighted. She confiscates his winnings and returns them to the sailors.

"That is theft," Leif protests.

"Then you have finally learned what it feels like."

### Chapter 2 — Wolves on the Water

On the sixth day, Mjöll sees movement behind a fog bank. Three longships are closing from two directions.

On the prow of the lead ship stands a tall warrior with a long sword and a plain round shield painted half black, half white. He is Rurik Shieldsplitter, the champion with a code. He has never killed a man who surrendered, never burned a house with people in it, never broken an oath. Men say he is the only one of Håkon's seven who could look his own mother in the eye.

Rurik's ships surround Fafnir's Bed. He calls across the water: give up the cargo, and every man aboard goes home alive.

The Solvik captain shouts back an insult about Rurik's mother. Rurik sighs and gives the order.

The sea battle is brutal. Ships grind together and grappling hooks fly. Astrid shoots from the Sea-Raven's mast, picking off enemy steersmen. Leif swings the Sea-Raven across the bow of one longship and snaps its oars like twigs. Fire arrows land on Fafnir's Bed's deck, and Ingrid and Ari run with buckets of seawater to put them out. Torvald holds the knarr's side against a boarding party, his hammer cracking shields.

Then Rurik boards the Sea-Raven himself and challenges Eirik to single combat. His men stop fighting to watch. So do Eirik's.

Rurik is the best pure swordsman Eirik has ever faced: calm, precise, never wasting a move. His shield is everywhere. They fight across the narrow deck, between the rowing benches, back and forth.

Rurik catches Raven-Feeder on his shield and freezes.

"Your father once carried that weapon."

Eirik stops. "You knew him?"

"I fought beside men who knew him." Rurik glances past Eirik, at Torvald on the deck of the knarr, and something passes over his face. "Torvald Grey-Cloak. So this is where you went."

Torvald says nothing.

Eirik raises his axe. "Then you know what happened to my father."

Rurik lowers his sword a little. "I know there are orders a warrior should refuse."

"Yet here you are."

Rurik's jaw tightens. He attacks again.

The duel lasts a long time. In the end, Rurik's honor is what beats him: he will not strike when Eirik slips on a wet oar, and waits for him to stand. Eirik does not make the same choice. When Rurik overextends, Raven-Feeder bites through his shield arm and then his mail.

Rurik sits down against the mast. He is dying and he knows it.

"Good," he says. "You fight for something more than revenge. I could see it." He coughs blood. "Ask the grey-cloak where he was the night before the first snow."

Then he dies.

The second of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

Rurik's men keep their word: they surrender, every one of them, because that was his rule. Eirik lets them keep one ship and sail home with their dead.

That night Eirik asks Torvald what Rurik meant.

Torvald looks at the sea for a long moment. "Rurik and I served the same king once, long ago. So did your father. So did Håkon. We all went different ways when the king died." He shrugs. "Dying men say strange things."

Eirik accepts it. He loves Torvald. He does not want to doubt him.

### Chapter 3 — The English Court

Fafnir's Bed arrives in Wessex nine days later, at a busy trading port where the River Itchen meets the sea.

England is another world. There are stone churches with bells. There are monks with shaved heads who cross themselves and run when they see Norse sails, which annoys Leif enormously because he wants to sell them furs. There are fortified towns called burhs, with earth walls and ditches, built by King Alfred to stop exactly the kind of people Eirik's crew look like. People speak a language close enough to Norse that you can almost understand it, which makes misunderstandings worse.

The cargo is delivered to King Alfred's reeve, a careful, polite man named Wulfstan who counts every arm-ring twice. The gifts are received. In return, Eirik is given a sealed letter of safe passage and a silver token with Alfred's name on it, recognizing that his men brought the cargo safely. Eirik does not think much of it. Later, it will open doors with every king who trades with England.

In the town, a farming family's little girl is burning with fever. The local healer has given up. Ingrid goes to help. Some of the townspeople do not want a Norse woman touching a Christian child. A priest shouts at her.

Ingrid does not leave. "A fever does not care which god you pray to."

She cools the child with wet cloths, makes willow-bark tea and stays awake with her for two nights. Leif, without being asked, goes from market to market finding the herbs she needs, haggling in broken English, and even pays full price for them without complaining once.

The fever breaks on the third morning. The girl's mother cries and kisses Ingrid's hands.

That evening Ingrid and Leif sit on the harbor wall. She has seen him do something with no hope of profit, and she does not know what to do with it.

Leif tells her, haltingly, about growing up hungry in the slums of Hedeby, a dock-rat stealing fish heads. How he swore he would never be poor again. How he still dreams about being hungry.

Ingrid says softly, "You count silver because you are afraid of having nothing."

Leif, stung, snaps back. "And you give it away because you have never had nothing."

She flinches as if he has slapped her. She gets up and leaves without a word.

Leif sits alone on the wall and calls himself every insult he knows.

### Chapter 4 — The Iron Circle

On the voyage home, Torvald and Eirik go through the sealed chest taken from Rurik's ship. Inside are letters written in runes on thin wooden slips and on parchment.

The letters reveal something none of them suspected. Håkon is not alone. A secret alliance of wealthy jarls, calling themselves the Iron Circle, is paying for his ships, weapons and mercenaries. They arrange marriages, buy hostages and bribe island lawspeakers. Their plan is to put every island under Håkon and then rule through him. One name appears again and again: Sigurd Stonehand.

There is also something stranger. Several letters mention old King Halfdan the Grey, dead for thirty years. One contains a single line Eirik reads three times.

Confirm the bloodline is ended before the North is crowned.

Eirik does not understand it.

Torvald does. His face goes grey. That night, when Eirik is asleep, Torvald takes three of the letters out of the chest and burns them in the ship's cooking fire. Only Mjöll sees him do it, and she does not know what it means.

### Chapter 5 — Six Islands Burning

On the last day of the voyage, Astrid climbs the mast and goes very still.

"Smoke," she says. "Everywhere."

Håkon has struck all six islands around his own: Jarnøy, Solvik, Kinn, Gulen, Fedje and Skorpa, all in the same week. He did not try to conquer them. He did something worse. He burned their harbors, sank their fishing boats, stole their grain right before winter and carried off hostages: sons and daughters of chieftains, held on Svartøy to guarantee obedience.

The message is simple: Anyone who helps Ravenhold will starve.

The next weeks are a blur of desperate work. The Sea-Raven pulls survivors from a burning harbor on Kinn. Eirik and Astrid hunt a band of raiders through Fedje's forests, the ones who stayed behind to loot. They escort boatloads of refugees to Ravenhold. They defend a signal tower on Skorpa against a last raid. Eirik rides Bjorn's grey horse from farm to farm across Gulen carrying wounded people to Ingrid.

What they see never leaves them. Homes full of smoke and rotting food. Cows wandering with no one to milk them, bellowing in pain. Dead dogs in the streets. Wounded men whose cuts have gone green and stinking because no one cleaned them; Ingrid has to cut away rotting flesh while Leif holds them down, and Leif throws up afterward every single time.

On Solvik, a mother and father dig through the ashes of their house for their missing son. They dig for two days. Eirik stays and digs with them. They do not find him. That night Mjöll finds him, alive, hiding in a sea cave with his grandmother's cat. It is the only good news in a month.

Ingrid works until she falls asleep standing up. Leif catches her and carries her to a bed. When she wakes, he is asleep on the floor beside it.

"I was wrong," he says, the moment her eyes open. "About what I said in England. You're not giving it away because you never had nothing. You're giving it away because you know what nothing is like for other people."

Ingrid looks at him for a long time. "That's the first wise thing you've ever said."

"Don't get used to it."

### Chapter 6 — The Council of Six

In the first frost, the six kings come to Ravenhold.

Ravenhold is not a village anymore. It has a harbor with twelve ships, a market, two forges, a healing house, three hundred people inside its walls and more camped outside. Refugees from all six islands have come there because they heard Ravenhold takes people in. Freydis has organized all of it: food rations, shelter, work.

The kings meet in Bjorn's longhouse. It is not friendly.

King Eystein of Solvik says war will destroy trade forever and they should pay Håkon whatever he asks. King Ragnvald of Kinn shouts that he will not kneel to a wolf. King Hallvard of Gulen recites three laws about the duties of neighbors. King Aslak of Fedje wants revenge, now, tonight. King Svein of Skorpa just wants his grain back before his people starve. King Hroald of Jarnøy is silent, because Håkon took his son Arvid as a hostage.

Eirik listens for a long time. Then he speaks.

"Håkon didn't attack you to conquer you. He attacked you to make you afraid of each other, and afraid of helping me. If each of us fights alone, he will destroy us one by one. If we stand together, he has to fight all of us at once."

He proposes an alliance for defense. They share ships, food, iron and warriors. An attack on one is an attack on all.

Bjorn backs him. So, surprisingly, does Eystein, after Eirik shows him the silver token from King Alfred. "If my merchants are known in Wessex as friends of the man who beat Rurik Shieldsplitter," Eystein says slowly, "perhaps trade will survive after all."

The kings agree. They choose Eirik to command the alliance's defense. He is not a king. His only power is the trust of six kings and the oaths they swear.

But there is a problem. Håkon's harbor on Svartøy is the strongest in the North. Three other islands, Vængr, Myrholm and Skalløy, refuse to join either side. Without them, the alliance cannot win.

### Chapter 7 — The Betrayal at Hedeby Sound

A week later, a letter reaches Torvald. It is brought by a ragged peddler and handed over in secret. It is wrapped around a faded blue hair ribbon.

Torvald knows the ribbon. It belonged to his daughter, Runa, who vanished in a raid nine years ago.

The letter is from Sigurd Stonehand. Runa is alive, it says, a thrall on one of Håkon's farms. She will be freed, safe and unharmed, if Torvald does one thing. When the alliance sends its refugee convoy south through the narrow channel called Hedeby Sound, he will light the signal fire on the third headland instead of the second.

Torvald does not sleep for three nights. He loves Eirik. He loved Ragnar. But he also believes, deep down, what Håkon believes: that the North has bled for thirty years because there was no strong king, and maybe a cruel strong king is better than endless war. And his daughter, his only child, might be alive.

The convoy sails: twenty ships full of refugees, grain and wounded, with the Sea-Raven and eight warships guarding them. Torvald is in charge of the signal fires along the coast that tell the fleet which channel is safe.

He lights the third fire.

The fleet turns into the narrow eastern channel, and enemy longships come out from behind the rocks on both sides. It is a trap.

What follows is the worst day of Eirik's life since he was nine. Enemy ships ram the slow refugee boats. Fire arrows set sails alight. The channel is so narrow that the fleet cannot turn around, and so shallow that ships keep running aground. Eirik and Leif fight to cut burning ropes, haul people out of the water and steer through rocks that Mjöll spots from above.

Ari Hauksson, the boy who wanted so badly to be a warrior, is on the deck of the Sea-Raven passing arrows to Astrid when a spear from an enemy ship takes him in the chest. Eirik holds him as he dies. Ari is trying to say something about his father. He never finishes.

Astrid is hit by an arrow in the shoulder and keeps shooting until she faints. The Sea-Raven is rammed and starts taking water. Seven allied ships are lost. More than eighty people die.

The survivors wash up on a rocky beach at nightfall.

Eirik walks along the beach looking for survivors. He finds Torvald standing alone by the shore, looking at the burning wrecks.

In Torvald's hand is a blackened signal torch. On the headland above them, the wrong fire is still burning.

Eirik understands everything at once.

"You led them here."

Torvald says nothing.

Eirik raises Raven-Feeder. His hands are shaking. "How many of them called you brother?"

Torvald answers quietly. "All of them."

Eirik swings. Torvald does not raise his hammer to defend himself. At the very last moment, Ingrid throws herself between them. The axe stops a finger's width from her face.

"If you kill him now," she says, "you'll never know why. And you'll become what he is."

Eirik stands there for a long time, breathing hard. Then he lowers the axe.

Torvald is disarmed, bound and taken prisoner for judgment by the alliance.

That night Eirik sits alone and thinks about how close he came. For the first time he wonders whether revenge has become bigger than the person he wants to be.

### Chapter 8 — Driftwood

The survivors limp back to Ravenhold. It is worse there.

While the fleet was away, Kettil Red-Hand attacked with three ships to earn back his wolf emblem. He knew the warriors were gone. He thought Ravenhold would be easy.

It was not. The walls held. Freydis organized the defense. Bjorn fought at the gate with his big axe and held it for an hour against Kettil's best. Old Hrefna threw pots of boiling fish oil from the wall. Even Old Skeggi the goat butted a raider off the walkway into the ditch.

But Kettil burned the harbor, three warehouses and the food stored for winter. And Bjorn took an axe blow across his left leg. He will live. He may never walk without a stick again.

Freydis meets Eirik at the gate. She is filthy with soot. Her hands are bandaged.

"You promised we would be safe."

Eirik has no answer.

Hauk is waiting on the pier. He sees Eirik's face and he knows before anyone says anything. He sinks down onto the wet planks. Eirik kneels beside him and puts a hand on his shoulder, and stays there until it is dark.

Later, Eirik climbs alone to the old birch on the hill. The lightning-split trunk has grown two new crowns, one on each side. He remembers making his first fire under its twin, alone and starving, ten years ago.

Sót lands beside him. Mjöll lands on his shoulder.

Home? she asks.

Eirik looks down at the smoking harbor.

"Not yet."

End of Act IV.

## Act V — The Broken Raven

Eirik's age: 24 to 25. Theme: building something is harder than destroying it.

### Chapter 1 — Ashes Again

Ravenhold calls an assembly. Half the town blames Eirik for bringing Håkon down on them. The other half says he is the only reason they are alive.

Hauk stands up. He has aged ten years in a month.

"My son followed you across the sea. He came home in a sailcloth."

Nobody breathes. Eirik does not defend himself. He does not explain about the trap, or Torvald, or the narrow channel. He stands and listens until Hauk has said everything he needs to say, and then he says, "I'm sorry. I'll carry it."

Hauk sits down. He does not forgive him. But he does not leave Ravenhold either.

The rebuilding begins, and it is nothing like the glorious wall-building of five years ago. There is not enough of anything. Every choice costs something. Timber for a new watchtower means three refugee families sleep in a barn for the winter. Rebuilding the harbor means the healing house waits. A new ship means less firewood in the coldest month.

Eirik learns that leading people is mostly choosing which of them will be disappointed.

### Chapter 2 — The Winter of Hunger

The winter is the worst in living memory.

Flood water got into the last grain store, and by midwinter the barley is grey with mould. Several families eat it anyway. They get sick. Then a sailor arrives from the south with the bloody flux, a disease of the gut that turns people's insides to water. It spreads through the crowded refugee houses like fire through thatch.

Ingrid takes command. The sick go into a separate hall. Everyone must wash their hands. Water must be boiled. The latrine must be moved away from the well, again. Soiled bedding must be burned, not washed in the stream that people drink from.

Many people refuse. They say sickness is a punishment from the gods. Old Hrefna says the gods want a blood sacrifice at the midwinter blot, and that a horse should be killed and its blood sprinkled on the sick. The two healers have a screaming argument in the middle of the market.

It is Eirik who finds the compromise. They hold the blot. A horse is sacrificed, as the old ways demand, its meat shared out at a feast for the whole town, which is the best thing that has happened to the hungry in weeks. Hrefna blesses the sick hall. And then, in front of everyone, Hrefna announces that the gods have told her they prefer their worshippers' pots to be boiled clean. After that, people boil their water.

A traveling merchant arrives selling a magical cure: a bottle of brown liquid with a dried frog in it, guaranteed by the gods of three countries. Leif is about to buy two bottles for Ingrid's hospital when she catches him.

"It was guaranteed," Leif says.

"By whom?"

"The merchant."

"The man selling it?"

"Yes."

"Leif."

"I am beginning to understand your objection."

The merchant is thrown out of Ravenhold. Old Skeggi the goat eats his frog.

The hunger gets worse. Then, in the coldest week of the year, Mjöll comes back from the coast screaming one word over and over. Big! Big! Big!

A whale has beached itself on the shore an hour north. It is the size of a longship.

The whole town goes out with axes, knives and sledges. Butchering a whale is the most disgusting work any of them have ever done. They climb inside it. They are soaked in blood and oil to the shoulder. The smell is so bad that birds will not land nearby. Leif slips on the blubber and slides into the open belly, and comes out covered head to foot in something no one can identify.

It also saves them. There is meat for two months and oil for every lamp.

Of course, there is a legal fight. A neighboring farmer claims the whale because it beached on his shore. King Hallvard of Gulen, who is staying in Ravenhold, recites the ancient whale law from memory: a whale belongs to the landowner, but anyone who helps cut it is owed a share, and in a famine winter, the hungry come first. The farmer gets a third. He is not happy. Everyone else eats.

One more case comes to Eirik that winter. A refugee family from Kinn cannot pay their debt to a Ravenhold merchant. By law, the father and his oldest daughter can be taken as thralls until it is paid. The merchant has every right.

Eirik remembers the girl in the slave pen at Kaupang. He pays the debt himself. Then he goes to the Thing and asks Ravenhold to agree to a new rule: no one who comes to Ravenhold for shelter can be made a thrall for debt. The vote is close and bitter. It passes.

It is the first law Eirik ever makes.

### Chapter 3 — The Raven Taken

In early spring, Mjöll does not come home.

Sót circles above Ravenhold all day, screaming. When Eirik reaches for Mjöll's mind there is only fear and darkness and the smell of leather.

A message arrives two days later on an arrow fired into the gate. It is from Håkon. His falconer, Ottar, has caught the white raven in a net. If Eirik gives himself up at Håkon's hunting camp on the mainland by the next new moon, the bird will be freed. If not, she will be plucked and roasted at Håkon's table.

The kings think Eirik is mad to care. They are planning a war, and he is worried about a bird.

But Mjöll was with him when he had nothing. She was the first voice he heard after the lightning.

Eirik goes himself, with Astrid and Leif. He does not tell the kings.

The hunting camp is fortified: a palisade, dogs, thirty guards and Håkon's hawks and falcons in long wooden mews. Eirik cannot use Mjöll's eyes. He has never felt so blind. He uses Sót instead, but Sót sees the world as a list of things to steal and people to hate, and his pictures are hard to read.

They get in at night through the camp's tannery yard. The tanning pits are full of animal hides soaking in a mixture of urine, dog dung and lime, which is how hides are softened. When the dogs come sniffing, the three of them have to climb into the pits and sink up to their chins.

Leif mouths silently into the darkness, I will never forgive you for this.

The dogs do not smell them. Nothing could smell anything over that pit.

Inside, they find more than the raven. There is a pen of eleven prisoners: farmers taken from the coast, waiting to be shipped to Svartøy as thralls. Eirik refuses to leave them. That costs time.

He finds Mjöll in a small wooden cage in the mews, with a hood over her head. She is thin and her feathers are falling out. When he takes off the hood, she does not recognize him for a moment.

"I am here," he whispers.

Then her thought comes, very small.

Home.

They get out with the raven and all eleven prisoners, but it takes the whole night. By dawn, Håkon's supply ships have sailed from the camp's harbor, loaded with grain and weapons for Svartøy. If Eirik had gone straight for the harbor, he could have burned them.

Saving the ones he loves has a cost. He knows it, and he would do it again.

When they get back to Ravenhold, smelling so horrible that the gate guards will not let them in until they have jumped in the sea, Ingrid looks at Leif and laughs until she cries. It is the first time anyone in Ravenhold has laughed in weeks.

### Chapter 4 — The Ring of Halfdan

Raven-Feeder's handle cracked in the tannery fight. Brokk takes the axe apart to fit a new one. This time he takes off the dark iron collar at the top of the handle, the one he has never touched.

Inside it, packed into a hollow in the wood, wrapped in oiled cloth, is a ring.

It is a heavy gold oath-ring, the kind that chieftains and kings swear on. Stamped into it is a seal: a crowned raven over three waves.

Brokk holds it up to the fire. "I've seen that seal. On old coins. That's the seal of King Halfdan the Grey."

Bjorn, leaning on his stick by the hearth, closes his eyes. "I wondered," he says. "When I found you with that axe. The way your father must have fought. I wondered if Ragnar had been one of the old king's men. I never asked, because I was afraid of what you'd do if you knew."

Only one person alive might know the truth. Brokk remembers an old warrior named Thrand who served in Halfdan's guard and now lives as a hermit on Gannet Rock, a lonely island of seabirds two days' sail north.

Eirik sails there with Leif. Gannet Rock is a tower of stone white with bird droppings, so many seabirds wheeling over it that the sky is noisy with them. Thrand lives in a turf hut near the top. He is ancient, half blind, and he keeps a pet puffin.

When Eirik shows him the ring, the old man begins to weep.

"Your mother had her father's eyes."

Eirik does not understand.

Then Thrand tells him everything.

King Halfdan the Grey ruled the whole North, mainland and islands, for thirty years. He had no sons. He had one daughter, born late in his life: Ylva. When Halfdan died, the great jarls fought over his kingdom. Ylva was the last of his blood. Any jarl who married her could claim the throne, and any jarl who wanted the throne for himself had to kill her.

One of Halfdan's most trusted guards smuggled her out of the burning royal hall. He hid her in a lonely farm on the coast. He fell in love with her. They married and lived as poor farmers for fifteen years, and nobody knew.

That guard was Ragnar. Their son was Eirik.

And Ragnar had a shield-brother, the man he trusted most in the world, who knew the secret: Håkon.

Håkon believed the North had bled for twenty years because of fights over Halfdan's crown. He believed that as long as one drop of Halfdan's blood lived, there would always be someone to fight over. So he decided to end the bloodline and make himself the only power left. Sigurd's Iron Circle paid for it.

Eirik's parents were not killed for tribute or by bad luck. They were murdered because of who his mother was.

Eirik sits on the cold stone floor of the hut for a long time.

"If I am the last of his blood," he finally says, "does that make me king?"

Thrand shakes his head slowly. "Blood may give a man a claim. It does not give him the right to be followed. Your grandfather knew that. He was a great king because people chose to follow him, not because his father was a king before him."

Now Eirik understands the line in the Iron Circle's letter. Confirm the bloodline is ended before the North is crowned. They were checking that he was dead.

His war is not just revenge anymore. It is a question that will matter long after Håkon is gone: what gives anyone the right to rule?

Old Thrand dies that summer, peacefully, in his sleep. The puffin stays on his roof for a week, then flies away.

### Chapter 5 — The Wing Isle

To beat Håkon, the alliance needs the three neutral islands. The first is Vængr, the Wing Isle.

Vængr's ruler, Jarl Jorund, is a thin, suspicious man who has kept his island free for thirty years by never trusting anyone. His household guard is the finest in the islands: two hundred swordsmen trained since childhood. Their captain is Svanhild.

Svanhild meets Eirik at the harbor with her hand on her sword hilt. She is tall, fair-haired, scarred across the knuckles, and looks at him as if measuring him for a coffin.

"Men say you defeat wolves with an axe and speak to ravens. I would rather know whether you keep your promises."

Jorund will not even hear Eirik out. But Håkon makes the decision for him. While Eirik is on the island, Skarde Bear-Maw, the berserker champion, lands on Vængr's north coast with sixty berserkers.

Berserkers are warriors who work themselves into a killing madness before battle. Skarde's men wear bear and wolf skins, howl like animals and bite the rims of their own shields until their mouths bleed. Some say they drink a potion made from red mushrooms. They feel no pain and never retreat. Skarde himself is enormous, wears a bear's skull as a helmet and fights with a Dane axe, a two-handed axe as tall as a man.

The berserkers burn two fishing villages. Jorund's guard is scattered along the coast. Eirik does not wait for an invitation. He, Astrid, Leif and twenty Ravenhold warriors march to defend the third village.

The fight runs through a forest settlement of log houses, through smoke and screaming. Svanhild arrives with thirty guards. She fights beside Eirik, and he sees that she is the best sword fighter he has ever seen: every cut is perfect, every step is placed. Astrid shoots berserkers who keep coming with three arrows in them.

The battle ends on Vængr's holmgang ground, a square of hazel posts by the shore where men fight formal duels to settle disputes. Skarde stands in the middle of it with his axe and roars a challenge.

Svanhild steps forward. Skarde's guards rush her, and she is fighting four at once. Eirik steps into the square instead.

Skarde is the strongest man Eirik has ever faced. His axe splits Eirik's shield on the first blow. He laughs and talks the whole time.

"I was there, you know. At the farm in the snow. I held the torches. Your father begged at the end. Squealed like a pig."

It is a lie. Eirik saw his father die. But the rage comes up through him like fire, and he does exactly what Skarde wants. He charges, swinging wildly, defense forgotten. Skarde's axe handle cracks him across the face and sends him sprawling. Stormguard does not come; he is too angry to reach it.

On his back in the mud, blood in his eyes, Eirik hears his father's voice. A blade may save your life, but wisdom decides when to draw it.

He breathes. He gets up slowly. He stops attacking.

He waits.

Skarde comes at him again and again, and Eirik gives ground, blocks, slips aside, lets the giant tire himself out on air. The madness that makes a berserker so terrible also burns him out. After a long time, Skarde's swings get slower. His breath rasps. Then he makes one huge, desperate overhead swing. Stormguard flares. Eirik steps inside it and drives Raven-Feeder up under the bear-skull helm.

The third of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

The remaining berserkers, their madness broken, flee to their ships.

Jarl Jorund comes to the holmgang ground and looks at the dead champion for a long time. Then he looks at the village that is still standing, and at Eirik, who has asked him for nothing.

"You defended my people without asking for tribute," he says. "I've never known anyone do that. Vængr will join your alliance."

Svanhild asks Jorund's permission to go with Eirik. Jorund gives it.

She tells Eirik plainly, "You fight well when you're calm. But your temper will kill you before your enemies do."

"I know," says Eirik.

"Good. Then I'll stay close until you've fixed it."

On the voyage back to Ravenhold, the greatest sword-fighter in the islands spends two days with her head over the side of the Sea-Raven, being horribly sick. Leif is so delighted he cannot stop smiling.

"Not a word," Svanhild says between heaves.

"I said nothing."

"You're saying it with your face."

### Chapter 6 — The Letter That Was Never Written

In early summer, Astrid catches one of Sigurd's messengers sneaking through the hills with letters for Håkon's spies. Under questioning, the frightened man tells everything he knows.

The letter that broke Torvald was a forgery. Sigurd wrote it himself.

Torvald's daughter Runa did not survive her captivity. She died of fever on a farm on Svartøy eight years ago. Her owner kept her hair ribbon. Sigurd's men bought it from him for a single silver coin, because they needed something to break a loyal man.

Eirik goes down to the cell where Torvald has been held for half a year. He tells him.

Torvald does not move for a long time. Then he bends over and makes a sound Eirik has never heard a man make.

When he can speak again, he tells Eirik the rest. There is more, and it is worse.

Torvald knew about Ylva. He was one of the guards who helped Ragnar smuggle her out of Halfdan's hall. He was Ragnar's friend too, as close as Håkon.

Years later, when Håkon started gathering power, Håkon asked Torvald where Ragnar was hiding. Torvald told him. He told himself Håkon only wanted to talk. He told himself that if Halfdan's line was ended, the North would finally stop fighting over the crown. He told himself a great many things.

Then he lost his nerve. He rode to Hrafnvik and warned Ragnar to run. He was the grey-cloaked man at the door, the night before the first snow.

"And then I rode away," Torvald whispers. "I could have stayed and stood beside him. He would have had one more shield. I chose to live." He looks up at Eirik. "I told myself one family might die so a thousand families could live."

Eirik's voice is very quiet. "One of those families was mine."

"I know."

Eirik walks out of the cell without another word. He orders Torvald held until all the rulers of the alliance can judge him together.

That night he goes to Bjorn and Signe. He does not say anything. Signe holds him the way she did when he was ten and did not speak for forty days.

### Chapter 7 — The Feast of Survivors

By midsummer, Ravenhold has rebuilt. The harbor is full again. The barley is green. Vængr has joined the alliance. The town decides it has earned one night of happiness, and holds a feast.

It is a good night. There is roast pig and fresh bread, the first in a year. There is music from bone flutes and a drum. Children play hnefatafl, the Viking board game of king and attackers, on boards scratched in the dirt. Brokk tells the story of his crushed hand again; tonight it was a sea serpent, which he wrestled for three days.

Astrid challenges Svanhild to an archery contest. Svanhild, who has never lost at anything, accepts. Her first arrow hits a goat. Fortunately it is Old Skeggi, who does not notice, and walks around for the rest of the night with an arrow stuck in his woolly backside like a feather in a hat. Her second arrow goes over the wall entirely.

Astrid laughs out loud, a sound almost nobody has heard. "Your sword cannot solve everything."

Svanhild draws her sword and points it at the target. "Give me a sword and I will prove otherwise."

She throws the sword. It hits the target dead center.

The crowd goes wild. Astrid concedes the point. They have been friends ever since.

Leif finds Ingrid away from the fires, by the harbor wall where the noise is quieter. For once he does not make a joke.

"I used to believe the world belonged to whoever could take the most."

"And now?"

"Now I think I would rather have someone waiting when I come home."

Ingrid takes his hand. Their first kiss is quiet, away from everyone, with the sound of the sea and the music in the distance.

Eirik sees them from the wall and smiles, and does not tell anyone. Freydis sees them too, and tells everyone within the hour.

For one night, everyone in Ravenhold is happy.

Then, near midnight, a small boat rows into the harbor. In it is a wounded messenger from Myrholm, the marsh island. Myrholm's queen has been accused of murder, her council is tearing itself apart, and another of Håkon's champions is on the island.

End of Act V.

## Act VI — The Nine Oaths

Eirik's age: 26 to 28. Theme: a kingdom is built from promises, sacrifice and the courage to trust again.

### Chapter 1 — The Marsh Queen

Myrholm is a world of water. Reed beds taller than a man. Black pools. Narrow channels that wind for miles and lead nowhere. Islands of solid ground where villages stand on wooden stilts. Mist every morning. Midges every evening. Leeches always.

Queen Thora rules it through a council of seven old families who have been feuding since before anyone can remember. She is about fifty, sharp-eyed and tired, with a twelve-year-old son, Prince Eyvind, who is far too curious for his own good.

Thora refuses Eirik before he finishes asking. "My people have survived by staying out of other men's wars."

The next morning, her oldest advisor, a man named Gest, is found dead in a channel with an arrow in his back. The arrow has grey goose fletching with a blue thread wound under it. It is Astrid's.

Astrid, Leif and Ingrid are arrested. Eirik is allowed to stay free only because Thora wants to watch him.

Astrid is furious. "Someone stole from my quiver. I count my arrows every night. Two went missing the night we arrived."

Eirik believes her. Nobody else does. The seven families are blaming each other, Thora's council is falling apart, and Myrholm is a breath away from civil war.

Eirik sends Mjöll over the marshes, day after day. She finds things nobody on the ground could see: a trail of trampled reeds leading to an old ruined watchtower, a boat hidden under cut branches, a camp where someone has been watching the queen's hall.

He needs a tracker. He goes to Thora and asks for Astrid's release, just for three days, with himself as surety: if Astrid runs or is guilty, Eirik will take her punishment. Thora is so surprised that anyone would offer this that she agrees.

Prince Eyvind sneaks out after them. When they find him, he refuses to go home. "You don't know the channels. I do." He is right. They take him.

The hunt through the marsh is slow and miserable. They wade waist-deep in brown water. The leeches are everywhere; Eyvind shows them how to burn them off with a hot knife without leaving the head in. In a peat cutting they find a body, black and leathery and perfectly preserved, a man who drowned in the bog hundreds of years ago with a rope still around his neck. Eyvind says the bog keeps everyone it takes. Leif, who has come along because he was bored in prison and Thora let him out too, says he has never wanted to be anywhere less.

At the end of the trail they find the killer. She is Eydis the Mire, the spear-champion. She does not fight like the others. She is a hunter who makes her prey come to her. She killed Gest with Astrid's arrow to turn the families against each other and against Eirik, so that Myrholm would tear itself apart and fall into Håkon's hand without a single ship.

She has turned the area around the old watchtower into a death trap. Pits full of sharpened stakes hidden under the water. Tripwires. Archers in the reeds. False trails that lead into sucking mud.

Astrid finds the tripwires. Eyvind knows where the solid ground is. Mjöll spots the archers. Eirik goes in.

The fight is in knee-deep water under the rotting watchtower. Eydis keeps Eirik at the end of her long spear, jabbing and retreating, throwing javelins from a bundle on her back. Every time he charges, the mud grabs his feet and she punishes him. He takes a cut on the shoulder, then the leg. The water around him turns red.

Then Eirik calls Sót. The black raven drops out of the mist straight into Eydis's face, screaming and clawing. For one heartbeat she raises her arm to protect her eyes. One heartbeat is enough. Eirik closes the distance and Raven-Feeder breaks her spear shaft, then her guard.

The fourth of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

In Eydis's camp they find Astrid's second stolen arrow, letters from Håkon, and a purse of silver marked with the Iron Circle's sign. It is all the proof anyone needs.

Thora releases the prisoners and apologizes to Astrid in front of the whole council. Then she asks Eirik why he risked his own freedom for someone who might have been guilty.

"Because she isn't," says Eirik. "And if I don't trust my own people, why would you?"

Myrholm joins the alliance. Thora gives them healers who know the marsh herbs, flat-bottomed boats that can slip through shallow water where no longship could go, and her island's greatest secret: the hidden channels along the coasts, including two that lead to the back of Svartøy.

Prince Eyvind asks to come with Eirik. Thora says absolutely not. Eyvind sulks magnificently, exactly as Ari once did. Eirik notices, and has to walk away for a minute.

### Chapter 2 — The Last Forge

The last neutral island is Skalløy, the Forge Isle, a place of quarries and furnaces where half the anchors, nails and ship fittings in the North are made. Its ruler, Jarl Gudmund, is a nervous, practical man who would like to join the alliance very much. But Håkon has sent him a message: if Skalløy sends one warrior to Eirik, Håkon will burn every workshop on the island and kill every smith.

Brokk volunteers to go and talk to Gudmund. His nephew Halvar lives on Skalløy. Brokk has not seen him in twelve years.

Eirik goes with Brokk, Astrid, Svanhild and a small escort.

The arrival is peaceful and almost too pleasant. Brokk sees Halvar across the harbor, a broad young man of twenty-five with a leather apron and burn scars on his forearms, and the old smith drops his bag and walks straight into his arms. Neither of them says anything for a long time.

Halvar has become a fine smith, with his own forge, three apprentices and a reputation. Brokk walks around his workshop with his hands behind his back, inspecting everything.

"You've made a good forge."

Halvar grins. "Better than yours."

"Then stop talking and prove it."

For three days the two of them work together while Eirik negotiates with Gudmund. Halvar has something special: a lump of black, heavy iron that fell from the sky in a fireball on a winter night two years ago. Sky-iron. It is harder than any iron from the ground, and almost impossible to work. They decide to make a hammer from it.

They work on Raven-Feeder too. Its edge is worn from years of fighting. Brokk and Halvar heat it, fold fresh Jarnøy iron into it, and quench it in seawater with a hiss and a cloud of steam.

While they hammer, Brokk talks. "Metal remembers every blow. So do men. The trick is learning what deserves to stay."

On the last night, sitting by the dying forge, Brokk finally tells Eirik the true story of his crushed hand. "I was twenty and drunk. I wanted to show off to a girl. I tried to lift the big anvil with one hand. It fell on me." He shrugs. "The truth is never as good as the stories. That's why I tell the stories."

Eirik laughs so hard he cries.

Gudmund agrees to join, if the alliance will defend Skalløy. Eirik gives his word.

### Chapter 3 — The Black Anvil

That same night, Håkon's answer arrives.

Bersi Black-Anvil, the most feared of the seven champions, lands on Skalløy with eighty of Håkon's toughest warriors. They carry axes, iron bars and buckets of pitch. They have not come to conquer. They have come to destroy the forges, so that the alliance can never repair its ships.

Bersi is a giant, bigger even than Gorm Stone-Lung, bald and black-bearded. He fights with a war hammer whose head is as big as an anvil. It is said he once killed a horse with one blow.

The alliance's small escort and Skalløy's few guards fight in the streets while Astrid and Svanhild get the smiths and their families out toward the hills. Eirik holds a narrow lane, then another, then another.

Bersi smashes through the gate of the forge complex. Halvar and his three apprentices are still inside, trying to save the tools.

Brokk stands in the doorway with a forging hammer in his good hand.

He is old. He has one working hand. He should not last a breath against a man like Bersi. He lasts much longer. He fights with forty years of knowing exactly where to put a hammer blow. He breaks the knee of one warrior and the jaw of another. He holds the doorway while Halvar drags the apprentices out the back.

Eirik comes running around the corner just in time to see Bersi's great hammer come down.

Brokk falls.

Eirik runs to him. Brokk grabs his arm with his crushed hand.

"Don't stop for me."

Bersi laughs. "Another old man dying for a boy who wants a crown."

Eirik stands up. The scar on his face burns like a hot coal. Stormguard comes, stronger than it has ever come.

And it is useless. It can show him every blow before it lands. It cannot reach back three heartbeats and put him in front of Brokk. It cannot undo what is done. Old Hrefna's words come back to him: It will not guard anyone else.

No power can prevent every death.

The battle with Bersi is the hardest fight of Eirik's life. Bersi never seems to tire. His hammer smashes Eirik's shield, then another one Eirik grabs off the floor. It crushes an anvil. It brings down a burning beam. They fight out of the burning forge, through the streets, up the path to the stone quarry on the cliff above the sea.

Eirik cannot block that hammer forever. Every time Stormguard saves him, he is weaker. On the quarry edge, he does what Bersi will not expect. He stops blocking. He steps backward onto a ledge of loose stone, and when Bersi swings his huge overhead blow, Eirik drops flat. The hammer hits rock with all of Bersi's weight behind it. The ledge cracks. Bersi stumbles forward. Raven-Feeder hooks his ankle, and Bersi Black-Anvil goes over the quarry edge into the sea.

The fifth of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

Only two of the seven are left: Ulf Two-Blades and Kettil Red-Hand.

### Chapter 4 — The Smith's Last Words

Eirik runs back down to the forge. Ingrid is already kneeling beside Brokk. She looks up at Eirik and shakes her head very slightly.

Brokk knows. He asks for Halvar.

Halvar kneels on his other side. Brokk puts his forging hammer, the one he has carried for forty years, into his nephew's hands.

"Build things that last longer than anger."

Then he turns his head toward Eirik.

"Your father gave you that axe. I only helped you carry it."

Eirik is crying. He cannot stop.

Brokk smiles weakly. "Don't make that face. I've spent sixty winters looking at ugly iron. I don't need to finish by looking at you."

He dies a little before dawn, with the forge fire still burning.

The alliance does not rush off to the next fight. They stay on Skalløy for nine days. Brokk is given a smith's funeral in the old way. A small boat is dragged up onto the beach and filled with dry wood. Brokk is laid in it in his leather apron, with his favorite tongs, a file and a little anvil at his feet, and a cup of ale in his good hand for the journey. His other tools are divided up as he asked: most to Halvar, some to the apprentices, his old leather apron to the Jarnøy smith he fought with for two years in Ravenhold.

Leif, who has never been quiet for a day in his life, says almost nothing all nine days.

Astrid leaves a bundle of arrows beside the boat.

Ingrid places a small folded cloth in Brokk's hand. Later she tells Leif what is in it: a little piece of iron, the first nail Brokk ever let her make, terribly bent.

Eirik speaks before they light the fire.

"He taught me that a weapon is not made strong by its sharpness. It is made strong by the hand that chooses when not to use it."

They light the boat. The smoke goes straight up.

Afterward, Halvar finishes Brokk's last work. Raven-Feeder gets its final reforging: iron from Jarnøy, a strip of sky-iron, and the iron of Brokk's craft. Halvar finishes the sky-iron hammer as well. It is black and heavy and rings like a bell. He names it Star-Fall. When Eirik tells Old Hrefna about it later, she only says: A star that falls and is beaten into a hammer.

Halvar comes back to Ravenhold with Eirik and becomes its master smith. Freydis, who is in charge of everything, is in charge of showing him where everything is. She takes a remarkably long time about it.

### Chapter 5 — The Judgment of Torvald

The alliance gathers at Ravenhold's Thing stones to judge Torvald. King Hallvard the Lawspeaker presides.

Torvald confesses everything, in front of all of them. That he told Håkon where Ragnar was hiding, nearly twenty years ago. That he rode away instead of standing beside his friend. That he lit the false signal at Hedeby Sound. That he believed in Håkon's peace: one strong ruler, no more succession wars.

"I thought obedience would save my daughter," he says. "It saved no one. I stopped asking what Håkon's peace would cost, and other people paid for it."

The rulers are divided. Aslak and Ragnvald want him put to death. Eystein wants him traded to Håkon for hostages. Hroald, whose son Arvid is still a hostage on Svartøy, says nothing at all. Hauk stands up at the back and says Torvald should be made to look at Ari's grave every day for the rest of his life.

In the end Hallvard says the decision belongs to the person Torvald wronged most: Eirik.

Eirik could kill him. Many people would cheer.

Instead, Eirik makes him swear a binding oath, on Halfdan's ring, in front of the whole Thing. Torvald will live. He loses his rank, his place on the council, his home in Ravenhold. He will give evidence against Sigurd and the Iron Circle. And he will do one thing more: when the alliance attacks Svartøy, he will lead a small team into the island's thrall pens, where Håkon keeps the hostages and the captives, and get them out. If he fails, or betrays them again, any man may kill him without penalty.

Torvald kneels and swears.

Eirik tells him, "Understanding why you betrayed us is not the same as forgiving you."

"I know," says Torvald. "I'm not asking you to."

### Chapter 6 — The Death of the Iron Circle

Sigurd Stonehand can see which way the wind is blowing. He sends Eirik a secret offer. The Iron Circle will abandon Håkon. It will give Eirik silver, ships and its support for his claim as Halfdan's grandson. All Eirik has to do is rule as the Circle advises.

Eirik sends back one line. "I have seen what your advice costs."

So Sigurd tries the other side. He goes to Svartøy and tells Håkon the Circle has lost faith in him. They want a new war leader, someone younger, someone who will win. Perhaps Håkon should step aside, for a generous price.

Håkon listens politely. Then he puts a bundle on the table: Sigurd's letter to Eirik, intercepted by Håkon's spies.

Sigurd stops smiling.

With nothing left to lose, he tells Håkon the truth. The Circle never wanted a king. They wanted a weapon. They paid for the raid on Ragnar's farm. They forged the letter that broke Torvald. They have been feeding both sides of this war so both would bleed, and the Circle could pick up the pieces.

"You were our sword," Sigurd says.

Håkon stands up. "Then you should have learned which end cuts."

He kills Sigurd in his own hall, in front of the Circle's other envoys. By the end of the month, Håkon has seized everything the Iron Circle owned: its silver, its ships, its hired warriors.

Håkon is not anyone's sword anymore. He is stronger than he has ever been. And he has nothing left to bargain with but war.

### Chapter 7 — The Nine Oaths

In autumn, every ruler in the alliance gathers at Ravenhold for a great war council. Ravenhold's longhouse is too small, so they meet in a field under a sail-cloth roof, with three hundred people listening at the edges.

Each island brings what it has. King Hroald brings Jarnøy iron and armored fighters. King Eystein brings merchant ships, grain and silver. King Ragnvald brings Kinn's sailors, the best in the North. King Hallvard brings the law, and lawspeakers to make the oaths binding. King Aslak brings Fedje's shipwrights. King Svein brings Skorpa's signal beacons and grain. Jarl Jorund brings Vængr's swordsmen. Queen Thora brings healers, marsh boats and her secret channels. Jarl Gudmund brings siege ladders, rams and ship fittings. Bjorn brings Ravenhold's warriors and leans on his stick and tells them all to stop wasting time.

The kings argue over who should command. None of them wants to give his warriors to another king.

Eirik does not ask for a crown. He holds up Halfdan's ring, and the whole field goes quiet, because by now everyone knows what it is.

"I won't ask you to swear to me because of my grandfather," he says. "Swear to each other. Your people remain yours. Your laws remain yours. But while Håkon threatens any one of us, we stand together."

One by one, nine rulers put their hands on the ring and swear: six kings, two jarls and a queen. Then Bjorn puts his big scarred hand on top of theirs, for the mainland.

The alliance now holds nine of the ten islands. Only Svartøy remains.

### Chapter 8 — A Wedding Before War

Leif and Ingrid decide to marry before the fleet sails. They both know the war might part them forever, and they do not want to wait for a peace that might not come.

First Leif has to agree the bride-price with King Hroald, as the old custom demands. Leif, who has bargained with merchants on three seas, is sure he can talk the price down. He walks into Hroald's tent confident. He comes out an hour later having agreed to pay twice what he planned, plus his best cloak, plus twenty years of free passage for Jarnøy cargo on the Sea-Raven. He is not entirely sure how it happened.

"He's a king," Ingrid tells him, patting his cheek. "You're a sailor. Did you really think you'd win?"

The wedding is held in Ravenhold at the end of autumn. The bride wears a crown of straw and late flowers. In the old way, the groom gives the bride a sword to keep for their future sons. Leif has no family sword, so he gives her one of his Twins, his short sword, which means from now on he will fight with one blade until she gives it back. Ingrid gives him a sword in return, as the custom asks, and a gift of her own.

Leif gives her a silver pendant shaped like a ship, beautifully made. Ingrid gives him a small leather medical kit.

Leif is confused. "A medical kit?"

"You are going to war. You are also an idiot."

"So this is a practical gift."

"Extremely."

Eirik stands beside Leif as his closest friend. Astrid and Svanhild give the toast together. It is a long, cruel and hilarious list of every terrible financial decision Leif has ever made, including the dice, the frog potion and the time he tried to sell a goat to a monk. Leif laughs harder than anyone.

There is a month's worth of mead, as tradition requires, and they try to drink it in one night. Freydis dances with Halvar four times, which does not go unnoticed. Old Skeggi eats the bride's crown.

The next morning Leif and Ingrid sit on the harbor wall watching the sun come up.

Leif says, "I promise I'll come back."

Ingrid shakes her head. "Do not promise what the sea cannot guarantee. Promise that you will try."

"I promise I'll try."

"Good. And bring back my husband's sword."

### Chapter 9 — Four Hundred

The alliance prepares the invasion.

About four hundred warriors will land on Svartøy, once the fleet has broken Håkon's blockade. Hundreds more sailors will stay with the ships, and garrisons stay home to guard the islands. Håkon has about five hundred warriors in his fortress, with more ships guarding the approaches. Kettil Red-Hand commands the land defenses. Ulf Two-Blades commands the fleet. Vigdis and Gorm, the left and right hands, guard Håkon himself.

The last weeks are busy. Eirik chooses commanders. He picks the ships. He studies Svartøy from the sky through Mjöll's eyes until his head pounds: the harbor, the cliffs, the bridge to the upper town, the great hall at the top. Halvar's forges ring day and night. Ingrid trains forty helpers to stop bleeding and splint bones. Thora's guides draw the secret channels in charcoal on sail-cloth.

Eirik talks with every one of his companions. Astrid asks him to look for a boy named Kári among the prisoners, if they get that far. Svanhild asks him to keep his temper. Leif asks him to stand far away from him in the battle so the arrows aimed at Eirik don't hit him by mistake. Eirik knows that means stay alive.

On the last night, Eirik visits Bjorn and Signe.

Signe holds him for a long time and says nothing.

Bjorn says, "I knew a frightened boy who could barely lift an axe."

Eirik smiles. "You taught him."

Bjorn shakes his head. "I taught him to split wood. The rest he learned from the people who trusted him."

Freydis gives Eirik a small square of cloth embroidered with a raven.

"For luck."

"Did you make it?"

"No. I stole it from your room."

They laugh, both of them, until Signe tells them to be quiet because the neighbors are sleeping.

Before dawn, the fleet of the nine oaths sails for Svartøy.

End of Act VI.

## Act VII — The Raven King

Eirik's age: 29. Theme: revenge ends a life. Leadership decides what happens afterward.

### Chapter 1 — Fire on the Water

The fleet of the nine oaths approaches Svartøy on a grey morning in early spring. Rain falls on dark water. There are sixty-two ships.

Eirik stands at the bow of the Sea-Raven. Leif is at the steering oar. Astrid is checking the string of Storm-Yew. Svanhild is checking her sword, and trying very hard not to be sick. Mjöll circles high above.

The night before they sailed, Eirik went to find Torvald. The old man was sitting alone at the edge of the camp, sharpening a hammer he no longer had the right to carry in the shield wall. Eirik held out Star-Fall, the sky-iron hammer, black and heavy.

"Halvar didn't want me to give you this," he said. "I'm not sure I want to either. But Brokk said metal remembers every blow. Give it a good one to remember."

Torvald took it and could not speak.

Now Torvald is on one of Queen Thora's flat-bottomed marsh boats with twenty volunteers and a Myrholm guide, slipping away from the fleet into the mist. They are heading for a hidden channel that leads to the back of Svartøy, where Håkon's thrall pens are.

Mjöll cries out, sharp and loud, in Eirik's mind.

Fire!

From the cliffs of Svartøy, hundreds of fire arrows rise into the rain. They are wrapped in rags soaked in burning pitch, and the rain does not put them out. They fall on the fleet. Sails catch. Barrels of supplies catch. On the Kinn ship next to the Sea-Raven, a sail goes up in a sheet of flame and men leap into the freezing sea.

Out of the mist ahead come Håkon's warships: thirty of them, chained together bow to stern in a line across the harbor mouth. A floating wall.

On the prow of the biggest ship, a sword in each hand, stands Ulf Two-Blades.

He has waited eight years for this.

The sea battle is a nightmare of fire, smoke and splintering wood. Ships ram each other. Oars snap. Men fight on burning decks and fall into the water between grinding hulls. Ragnvald's Kinn sailors steer as if they can feel the water. Astrid's archers rake the enemy decks. Fedje's ships carry big iron hooks that tear away the chains between Håkon's ships.

Leif knows these waters from his smuggling days. He takes eight ships around the end of the enemy line, through a passage so close to the rocks that the Sea-Raven scrapes paint off her side. Thora's marsh boats slip through a shallow channel too narrow for Håkon's big warships to follow, and attack the chained line from behind.

The floating wall breaks.

But Ulf's flagship swings around and rams the Sea-Raven amidships.

### Chapter 2 — Two Blades, One Sea

Ulf leaps across onto the Sea-Raven with twenty of his best men.

Leif meets him first. Leif still has only one of his Twins, his long seax. In his other hand he carries the sword Ingrid gave him at their wedding.

"A matched pair," Leif says. "Husband and wife. Want to meet them?"

The two of them fight up and down the rowing benches, both with two blades, both fast, both showing off. For a little while it is almost beautiful. But Ulf is better. He is faster than anyone alive except Vigdis Quickblood. He opens a long cut across Leif's shoulder and kicks him back over a bench.

That is long enough for Eirik to get there.

The final duel goes across the decks of both ships, locked together by the ram, both of them burning. Ulf has changed in eight years. Every trick he used on Jarnøy, he still has, and now he has counters for Eirik's counters. He talks the whole time.

"All these years, I wondered whether the farmer had learned to fight."

"All these years, I wondered whether you would stop running."

Ulf laughs, but his eyes are cold. They fight over the ram itself, a narrow beam above the black water. Then back onto the Sea-Raven. Stormguard flares three times. Eirik is bleeding from four places.

Ulf goes for the kill: a scissor strike with both swords. Sót comes screaming down out of the smoke and hits him in the face. For one heartbeat Ulf turns his head.

Eirik catches both swords on his shield, twists, and Raven-Feeder finds the gap under Ulf's arm.

Ulf staggers back to the rail and sits down on it. He looks almost surprised.

"I didn't run," he says.

"No," says Eirik. "You didn't."

Ulf Two-Blades falls backward into the sea.

The sixth of Håkon's seven champions is dead.

Leif is sitting against the mast, holding his shoulder, very pale. He opens Ingrid's little leather medical kit with his teeth and starts bandaging himself.

"A practical gift," he says weakly. "She was right. Never tell her."

Eirik helps him up. Ulf's ship is sinking. Without their commander, Håkon's remaining ships scatter. The fleet of the nine oaths breaks through into Svartøy's harbor.

### Chapter 3 — The Black Shore

About four hundred alliance warriors wade ashore through cold surf onto the black volcanic sand of Svartøy.

Ahead of them, the island rises in terraces. A harbor town of burned-out warehouses. A road climbing through rocky slopes. A ravine crossed by a long wooden bridge. Above it, the upper town behind a palisade. At the very top, Håkon's great hall, its roof carved with wolves.

About five hundred of Håkon's warriors hold the slopes, the bridge and the hall.

Eirik divides his army. Astrid commands the archers. Svanhild leads the main shield wall. Leif, bandaged and swearing, brings reinforcements up from the ships. Ingrid sets up a field hospital in a fisherman's boathouse by the landing. The kings and jarls each hold part of the line: Hroald's armored Jarnøy men in the center, Jorund's Vængr swordsmen on the right, Ragnvald's sailors on the left.

The fighting on the slopes is not like a song. Nobody fights in heroic single combats while the armies wait. It is shoving, stabbing, falling, getting up again. Shield walls crash together and grind. Men slip in mud and blood. A Vængr captain falls and his men panic until Svanhild bellows at them to hold. Wounded men call for their mothers. Some enemy warriors fight to the death. Others throw down their weapons and run.

Eirik can be in only one place at a time. Twice he has to choose between rescuing a cut-off group of allies and pushing on toward the bridge. Both times he goes back for the trapped men. It costs time. It also means that when the men of the nine islands look at him, they know he will come for them.

### Chapter 4 — The Red-Hand Returns

At the bend in the road below the bridge, Håkon's reserve is waiting. Its commander sits on a black horse.

Kettil Red-Hand.

He is twenty years older than the young raider who chased a boy through the snow. His face is lined. His ruined right hand still wears its iron buckler. He has a new scar on his face from Bjornstead, and his wolf emblem is back on his cloak, earned by burning Ravenhold's harbor.

He has spent twenty years living with the shame of losing to a boy, and then to a farmer. It has made him harder, more careful and more dangerous.

"I should have killed you when you were a child."

Eirik answers, "You tried. Twice."

Kettil gets off his horse. They fight on the muddy road while the battle roars around them.

Kettil fights dirty. He throws mud. He has a knife hidden in his buckler and another in his boot. He feints with the axe and stabs with the knife. He does not care about honor or glory. He wants to live, and he wants Eirik dead.

But Eirik is not nineteen anymore. He has fought Arne and Rurik, Skarde and Eydis, Bersi and Ulf. He knows every trick Kettil has, because he has seen worse. He turns the knife, breaks the buckler strap, and knocks the axe from Kettil's left hand.

Kettil falls to his knees in the mud. He looks up at Eirik, and for a moment he is just a frightened, ageing man.

"Will killing me bring them back?"

Eirik is quiet for a long time.

"No."

The noise of the battle seems far away.

"But you will kill no one else for him."

Eirik ends it.

The seventh and last of Håkon's champions is dead. All seven have fallen.

Word spreads along the enemy line like a crack through ice. The champions are gone. Many of Håkon's hired warriors, the ones paid with the Iron Circle's silver, decide they have not been paid enough to die. They start drifting back toward the upper town. Some throw down their weapons.

### Chapter 5 — The Pens

On the far side of the island, Torvald's twenty land from the marsh boat in a hidden cove and climb up a goat path to Håkon's thrall pens.

The pens are long, low stone buildings that stink of unwashed people, rotten straw and despair. More than a hundred thralls are held there, along with the hostages Håkon took from the six islands: the sons and daughters of chieftains, among them Prince Arvid of Jarnøy, thin and filthy but alive.

The guards are many more than twenty. Torvald does not care. He goes through the gate with Star-Fall and fights like a man who has finally found something worth dying for. The sky-iron hammer breaks shields, doors and chains. His volunteers fight beside him. Seven of them die. Torvald takes a spear in the side and keeps going.

They break every lock. Arvid takes a dead guard's sword and starts arming the others. The freed thralls pick up whatever they can find: axes from the woodpile, spears from the guards, stones. More than a hundred angry people who have nothing left to lose follow Torvald down the hill, straight into the rear of Håkon's army holding the bridge.

At the edge of the field, Astrid sees the thralls pouring down the slope. Her eyes go to a skinny young man near the front with a brand on his cheek and a fishing gaff in his hands. He is seventeen now. He was eight when she last saw him. She knows him anyway.

"Kári!"

He turns. He stares. Then he drops the gaff and runs.

Astrid, who has not cried in front of anyone since her family died, holds her little brother in the middle of a battlefield and sobs.

Torvald sits down against a rock, holding his side, and watches them. He thinks about his own daughter, who did not come home. He finds he can bear it a little better.

### Chapter 6 — Vigdis, the Left Hand

Håkon's army holds two last positions: the long bridge across the ravine, and the gate of the great hall.

Håkon sends his two bodyguards to stop Eirik.

The first is Vigdis Quickblood, the Left Hand.

She waits for him alone on the bridge. She is small, wiry, grey-eyed, with a twin seax in each hand and no shield. She does not shout or boast. She simply waits.

The moment Eirik steps onto the bridge, she is on him. She is faster than Ulf. Faster than anything. She does not stand and trade blows. She darts in, cuts, and is gone before he can swing. She comes from the left, then the right, then low, then from behind. In ten heartbeats she has cut him in three places.

Then fire arrows from Håkon's last archers set the bridge alight. Smoke rolls across it. Planks start to fall away into the ravine. A railing collapses. The safe ground shrinks.

Vigdis sees the flash in his scar when Stormguard saves him.

"So the stories are true."

"Most stories grow with every telling."

"Then let us see what remains when you bleed."

She is too fast to chase and too fast to react to. Every time he answers her last attack, she is already making the next one. Eirik starts to understand that he will never catch her by being quicker.

So he stops trying. He stops reacting to every movement. He breathes, the way he learned at the holmgang ground on Vængr. He watches the rhythm of her feet instead of her blades. She always steps twice before she strikes. Always the same rhythm, left-left, right-right.

Left-left. He raises the shield before the blade comes, and it lands right on it. Right-right. He turns, and her seax hisses past his ear. She frowns. For the first time, she hesitates.

On the next left-left, he does not block. He steps in.

Vigdis Quickblood dies on the burning bridge with both blades still in her hands. She was loyal to Håkon to the last, the only cause she had ever known.

Eirik limps across. Behind him, a section of the bridge falls into the ravine in a shower of sparks. Svanhild and his warriors cross on the beams that remain.

### Chapter 7 — Gorm, the Right Hand

Beyond the bridge, in front of the great hall's gate, stands Gorm Stone-Lung, the Right Hand.

He is almost twice the size of an ordinary man. He wears three layers of mail over thick leather. He carries a huge round shield bound in iron and a club with an iron head the size of a child. He has fought for Håkon for thirty years and has never been beaten.

The alliance's best warriors try him first. Gorm sends them flying. A Jarnøy champion's axe bounces off his shield. A Vængr swordsman is knocked down so hard he does not get up. Svanhild attacks him with every skill she has, and Gorm simply takes her cuts on his mail and shield and knocks her off her feet with one swing of his shield.

Eirik tells her to pull the wounded back and hold the bridge end. Then he faces Gorm himself.

This is not a fight of speed. It is a fight of endurance. Gorm's attacks are slow, but each one could crush a horse. Eirik's attacks barely dent that shield. Every time he hits the mail, it is like hitting a wall.

So Eirik does what Brokk would do: he looks for the weak points. He makes Gorm swing, and swing again, and swing again, until the big man's breath starts to rasp. He hits the shield's edge until the iron rim cracks. He cuts at Gorm's exposed fingers, his knees, the back of his ankle. He leads Gorm onto the slippery steps of the hall, where the giant's weight works against him.

At last Gorm throws away his split shield and takes the club in both hands. Now he is faster and more dangerous than ever, but he is also open. He swings a crushing overhead blow. Stormguard flares, almost too weak now to answer. Eirik throws himself sideways. The club smashes the stone step where he was standing into gravel.

Raven-Feeder comes around from the right and finds the gap at the side of Gorm's neck, between helmet and mail.

Gorm sinks to his knees, then falls back against the great wooden gate of the hall.

With his last breath he says, "Håkon is waiting."

Eirik pulls Raven-Feeder free.

"Then I should not keep him."

The gate is pushed open.

### Chapter 8 — The Hall of Wolves

Eirik goes in alone. He tells the others to stay outside. Svanhild argues. Leif argues. Eirik just shakes his head.

The great hall is quiet. The sound of the battle outside becomes a distant roar. A long fire burns down the middle of the floor. Weapons line the walls on racks: swords, spears, axes, bows, shields. Old faded banners hang from the beams, some of them carrying the crowned raven of King Halfdan.

Håkon sits alone in a carved high seat at the end of the hall. He is grey, broad, sixty years old and still powerful. In front of him on the table lies a broken gold oath-ring, snapped in two. It is the ring Ragnar, Håkon and Torvald swore on as young men when they became Halfdan's guards.

Håkon speaks first.

"You have your grandfather's eyes."

"You killed his daughter."

Håkon looks down. "Your mother was a good woman."

"Do not speak of her."

"I knew her before you were born."

Eirik raises his axe. "Then you knew exactly what you were doing."

Håkon stands. For the first time, he tells Eirik why.

After Halfdan died, the North tore itself apart. Jarls fought jarls for the crown. Farms burned. Trade stopped. Men were hanged for backing the wrong claimant. Children starved in winters that should have been easy. Håkon saw all of it. He came to believe that as long as anyone of Halfdan's blood lived, some ambitious jarl would always use them to start a new war. So he decided to end the bloodline, take the power himself, and rule the North with an iron hand until it learned to stop fighting.

He killed Ragnar and Ylva to prevent a greater war. He failed to kill their son.

And now the boy has united the very rulers Håkon wanted to control.

"You think you are different from me," Håkon says. "Look outside. Hundreds of men have died because you came here."

"They came because they chose to. Your men came because they feared what you would do if they refused."

Håkon laughs, bitter and tired. "And when your allies disagree with you? When they refuse your laws? When they turn their swords on each other?"

Eirik does not pretend to have every answer.

"Then I will have to earn their trust again."

Håkon shakes his head slowly. "You sound like your father."

"He was a better man than either of us."

Håkon reaches for his sword and shield.

"Then let us see whether his lessons can save you."

### Chapter 9 — The Ironwolf

The last fight of the war happens in four parts.

The sword of the guardsman. Håkon begins with sword and shield, the weapons of Halfdan's guard. He fights like the perfect warrior of the old songs: every block clean, every counter punishing. He reads Eirik's attacks before they begin. He is better than any of his champions.

He catches Eirik's axe on his shield and smiles. "Your father always struck from the left."

Eirik answers, "He taught me to change." The next blow comes from the right and splits Håkon's shield.

The weapons of war. Håkon throws away the broken shield and moves to the weapon racks along the walls. He fights with a spear, then an axe, then a sword again, switching whenever Eirik gets used to one. He throws a bench into Eirik's legs. He kicks the hearth fire and sends coals into his face. He uses every trick of forty years of war, and the hall itself, against him.

Wolf's Judgment. At the end of the hall, Håkon takes down his own weapon: a great war hammer with a head of black iron ringed with short, cruel spikes. It is the hammer that killed Ragnar.

The first blow smashes Eirik's shield to pieces. The second takes a chunk out of the wooden pillar beside his head. In the fighting, an iron brazier is knocked over. Burning oil spreads across the floor. The banners catch fire. Smoke fills the roof. Burning rafters crack overhead.

Håkon brings the hammer down in a blow that would kill anyone. Stormguard flares one last time. Eirik sees it coming, slips aside, and the spikes tear his cloak instead of his chest. Then the scar goes cold. The power is empty. He has used it all.

From here on, there is no magic. There is only what he has learned.

The last oath. Both men are badly wounded. Both are slowing down. Every move costs them. The hall is burning around them.

Håkon's hammer catches Raven-Feeder's handle and snaps the wood just below the head. But Brokk's iron collar holds, and the axe head stays on. Eirik is left with an axe a hand shorter than it should be.

He remembers a summer day on a woodpile, his father laughing.

A blade may save your life, but wisdom decides when to draw it.

He stops attacking. He waits.

Håkon raises Wolf's Judgment for the final blow, putting everything he has left into it.

Eirik does not dodge. He meets the blow with the iron collar of the axe, turns his whole body with the force of it the way Bjorn taught him to split a log, letting the hammer's power carry him around in a full circle, and strikes from the right.

Håkon falls.

The great hall is silent except for the crackle of burning wood.

Eirik kneels beside him. Håkon's eyes go to the broken oath-ring on the table.

"Will they remember me as a monster?"

"They will remember what you did."

Håkon breathes in, slow and wet. "And what will they remember of you?"

Eirik looks toward the open doorway. Beyond it, in the smoke and grey light, are the warriors of nine islands and the thralls Torvald freed.

"That is still mine to decide."

Håkon Ironwolf dies.

Eirik picks up both halves of the broken oath-ring and puts them in his pouch. Then he walks out of the burning hall before the roof comes down.

### Chapter 10 — What the Dead Leave Behind

When Eirik walks out of the hall, the battle is over.

Håkon's last warriors throw down their weapons. Svanhild orders the fighting stopped, and is obeyed. In the field hospital, Ingrid and Thora's healers work on wounded men from both armies. Astrid and Kári carry a wounded enemy warrior down the hill to them. Torvald, bandaged and grey-faced, sits among the freed thralls and gives them water. Prince Arvid finds his father, and King Hroald, who has not wept in front of anyone in twenty years, holds his son and does not let go.

Leif finds Eirik sitting on the steps of the burning hall. For once, he has no joke.

"Is it finished?"

Eirik looks across the battlefield. Smoke. Dead men. Living men looking for their friends among the dead.

"No."

Leif looks at the bodies.

Eirik continues. "The killing is."

They spend three days on Svartøy. They bury the dead of both sides. They count the living. Eighty-three of the alliance's four hundred have died. More than two hundred of Håkon's. Eirik walks through all of it. He talks to the wounded. He helps search for the missing. He sits with Hauk, who came on this voyage for Ari's sake, while Hauk carves Ari's name on a stone by the shore.

Victory does not erase what war costs. It only means it stops.

### Chapter 11 — The Crown of the North

Six weeks later, every ruler of the ten islands gathers at Gulen's law-rock for the greatest Thing anyone has ever seen. Svartøy sends men too, chosen by its own people, frightened of what will happen to them.

They argue for four days.

Some want Eirik to be an all-powerful king like Halfdan was. Some want each island to go its own way now Håkon is gone. A few say quietly what they all fear: that Eirik will simply become another Håkon.

Halfdan's ring proves Eirik's bloodline. Old Thrand's sworn testimony, written down by Hallvard's lawspeakers before he died, proves it. Torvald, standing before the whole Thing in disgrace, proves it again.

But Eirik stands on the law-rock and refuses to say that his blood makes him worthy.

"My mother was born to a king. My father died a farmer. The people who raised me owned no throne." He looks across the crowd at Bjorn, leaning on his stick, and Signe beside him. "They taught me that a home is not what a man takes. It is what he protects."

He raises Raven-Feeder, its broken handle replaced with fresh ash wood by Halvar.

"I will not ask you to kneel because of my blood."

He lowers the axe.

"I ask you to stand beside me because of what we can build."

The rulers vote. Eirik becomes High King of the North, the first in thirty years. But it is a different kind of kingdom from Halfdan's. Every island keeps its own ruler and its own laws. The great Thing meets at Gulen every summer, and even the High King must answer to it. Disputes between islands go to the law, not to the sword.

His first laws as High King are short. No one may be made a thrall for debt anywhere in the North. Every captive taken by Håkon is free. Svartøy will not be plundered; its people will choose their own jarl at their own Thing, under the same law as everyone else.

It does not solve everything. There will be feuds. Some jarls will grumble. Some will plot. The North has not become a peaceful paradise in a day. But it has a beginning.

The crowd at the law-rock begins to chant. Somebody started it as a joke, because of the two birds sitting on the High King's shoulders. By the end of the day, the whole North is saying it.

EIRIK RAVENSSON. THE RAVEN KING.

End of Act VII.

## Epilogue — The Old Birch

Twelve years later. Eirik is 41. Theme: let our children inherit something better than our wars.

### Ravenhold

Ravenhold has become the busiest harbor in the North. The old palisade still stands, but the town has spilled far beyond it: houses, workshops, gardens, a market where you can hear six languages before breakfast. Ships come in from Wessex, Frisia, Dublin and the far eastern rivers. Former enemies trade side by side. A Svartøy fish-seller and a Jarnøy iron-merchant argue over prices every morning and drink together every evening.

At the edge of town stands a row of carved stones: one for every person from Ravenhold who died in the war. Ari Hauksson's is the first. Every morning an old grey-bearded man walks there, sweeps the leaves off it, and stands for a while. It is Torvald. Nobody makes him do it anymore. For years, Hauk refused to look at him. Last spring, Hauk came and stood beside him without a word. This spring, he brought two cups of ale.

Torvald lives in a small house by the children's hall, where the orphans of the war are raised. He teaches them to swim, to read the weather and to carry a shield. He never teaches them to fight angry. Star-Fall hangs on his wall. He has never used it again.

In Brokk's old workshop, Halvar is master smith. His forge makes fewer swords these days and more plough-blades, nails, pots and ship fittings. Above the door hangs Brokk's old forging hammer.

A young apprentice is hammering too hard and bending everything. Halvar stops her.

"Anyone can learn to strike iron. Learn first what the thing you make is meant to do."

On the anvil sits Halvar's proudest work: the Thing-Ring. He melted down the two halves of the broken oath-ring from Håkon's hall, the ring Ragnar, Håkon and Torvald once swore on, together with King Halfdan's old ring. From them he made one great new ring. It does not belong to the High King. It lives at Gulen's law-rock, and every ruler, the High King included, swears on it each summer before the Thing. Eirik insisted on that.

Astrid runs the hunters' and scouts' training on the mainland. Her brother Kári is her best student, though she will never tell him so; he married a girl from Myrholm and has a son who already tries to steal his aunt's arrows. Astrid has never married. She says she has not yet met a man who can outshoot her, and she is not in a hurry.

Svanhild trains the alliance's young warriors in the fields above town. Discipline, footwork, patience. She is still the finest sword in the North. She is still seasick. Every time she sails to the summer Thing, Leif waits at the dock to watch her board.

Old Hrefna is ninety and claims to be a hundred. She and Ingrid still argue about goat urine. They run the healing house together.

Bjorn died two winters ago, in his sleep, after a big supper and a long argument with Freydis about the price of salt. He is buried in a mound under the old birch on the hill, with his big bearded axe, a ham and a barrel of ale, because he said he was not going to walk into Odin's hall hungry. Half the North came to his funeral. Three kings carried his coffin. Signe lives with Freydis now and still runs the household, and also, quietly, the town.

And Old Skeggi the goat is long gone. His great-grandson, also called Skeggi, eats rope, shoes and sails, and last month ate a letter from the King of the Danes. Leif tried to have him put on trial. Hallvard the Lawspeaker ruled, again, that a goat cannot be sued.

### Leif's House

Leif and Ingrid live in a big house near the harbor, with a view of the ships. Leif owns four of them now. He is a respected merchant and the best captain in the North. He tells everyone he is fantastically rich.

Ingrid manages all his accounts, because he is still terrible at counting.

Their daughter Liv is ten, with her mother's eyes and her father's grin. She is sitting on the table eating an apple and looking at her father with deep suspicion.

"Father. Is it true you once lost an entire ship in a game of dice?"

Leif waves a hand. "That is an exaggerated story."

Ingrid walks in with a basket of herbs. "He lost two."

Liv laughs so hard she falls off the table. Leif catches her before she hits the floor, the way he once caught her mother in a hospital on Solvik.

On the wall above the hearth hang the Twins, both of them, side by side. Ingrid gave back the short sword the day Liv was born. "For our daughter," she said. "Since we did not have sons, and since she is going to be much more frightening than any son."

Leif, who grew up a starving dock-rat in Hedeby stealing fish heads, stands in his own warm kitchen with his wife and daughter laughing at him. For a moment he has to turn and look out the window, so nobody sees his face.

### The Last Lesson

That evening, the High King of the North walks up the hill behind Ravenhold to the old birch.

The lightning-split trunk has grown into two great crowns, leaning together. Bjorn's burial mound sits beneath it, green with grass. Below lie the fields, the town, the harbor, and beyond them the sea and the dark shapes of the ten islands.

With Eirik is Freydis's son, Little Brokk. He is nine, the age Eirik was when the wolves came over the ridge. He has his mother's sharp tongue and his father's big hands, and he is carrying a wooden axe Halvar carved for him.

He has been asking questions all the way up the hill.

"Uncle, how many enemies have you killed?"

Eirik looks out over the fields.

"More than I ever wanted."

"Did that make you a great king?"

Eirik smiles, a little sadly.

"No."

"Then what did?"

Eirik looks down the hill. A farmer is helping his neighbor mend a broken fence, both of them laughing about something. By the stream, a group of children, some from Svartøy, some from Jarnøy, some from Ravenhold, are sharing a loaf of bread.

He kneels down beside the boy.

"Helping people build a life where they don't have to fight for every little thing."

Little Brokk thinks about this for a long time.

"Then why do we learn to use an axe?"

Eirik takes the wooden axe from him and turns it over in his hands, the way his father once did by a woodpile on the last warm day of autumn.

"Because sometimes the world forgets how to be kind."

He puts it back in the boy's hands.

"But remember what your hands are for when you're not fighting."

Mjöll died last winter, very old, with her feathers gone grey and her head on Eirik's hand. Sót, older still and as rude as ever, refuses to leave the birch and screams at anyone who walks under it.

Now a young raven drops out of the branches and lands on the stone at the foot of Bjorn's mound. It is black, except for one single white feather on its wing. It is Mjöll's granddaughter, hatched this spring in the old birch.

It cocks its head and looks at Eirik.

And very faintly, so faint he is not sure he has not imagined it, a small voice like wind in feathers speaks in his mind for the first time.

Home.

Eirik looks up. For a moment he is nine years old and his father is laughing on a woodpile. The same tree. The same sound of leaves in the wind. The same lesson, passed on.

The view rises above the hill, above the farms, above the town and the harbor, out over the sea and the ten islands, as a raven would see it.

And Eirik's voice speaks over it all.

"My father gave me an axe. My mother gave me a life. Bjorn and Signe gave me a home. My friends gave me something worth defending.
